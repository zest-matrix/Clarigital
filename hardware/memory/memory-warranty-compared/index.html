What a memory &ldquo;lifetime warranty&rdquo; actually promises

Almost every memory brand advertises a lifetime warranty, and almost none of them means the same thing by it. One ties it to an industry norm, one to how long the part stays on sale, one to how long the company keeps making it, and one to whatever the company later decides. One brand grants it on every module it sells and never defines the word at all. Here is what each of the six actually promises, what quietly voids it, and whether switching on the speed you paid for is one of those things. Three reading levels, every clause quoted from the company that wrote it.

1.One word, five meanings

SimpleStart here

Nearly every memory brand advertises a lifetime warranty. It is the single most repeated promise in the category, and it is worth knowing that the brands do not agree on what the word means.

BrandWhat “lifetime” is tied to
KingstonAn industry norm 1
PatriotAn industry norm on one page, its own production run on another 2 3
PNYHow long the part stays on sale 4
Silicon PowerWhatever Silicon Power later decides 5
LexarUse of the product; ten years in Germany and other regions where lifetime cover does not apply 6
CorsairNot defined anywhere 7

None of the six ties a memory module to your lifetime. Patriot says so outright: the warranty "does not refer to the lifetime of the original purchaser." 2 Lexar’s global document comes closest, tying some non-DRAM lines to "As long as owned by original purchaser" 8.

DeeperThe detail

Take the definitions in the companies’ own words, because the differences are not stylistic.

Kingston: "Product lifetime is defined as the normal time expectancy for the use of products in the industry." 1 The clock is set by what the industry considers a normal service life, not by anything Kingston controls or publishes.

PNY ties it to the shelf: "Lifetime is defined as the lifetime of the product on the market." And it spells out the consequence: "Outdated technology is not covered by the Lifetime Warranty if the item is no longer available on the market as a new product." 4 When PNY stops selling your kit, the cover ends.

Silicon Power hands itself the decision: "For limited Lifetime Warranty products, the warranty service starting from the original purchase date until the end of product life cycle. (The end of product life cycle is determined by SP)." 5 The grammar and the capitalisation are as printed.

Lexar defines it by the product’s use rather than its market: "“Limited lifetime warranty” means that Lexar will provide repair, replacement, or refund services free of charge if quality problems arise during the use of the product (shipping costs not included)." 6

Corsair is the outlier, and not in the direction you would expect. Its warranty schedule grants "Limited Lifetime" to all DRAM memory modules — the only line in its entire product range at lifetime cover, against two years for graphics cards and ninety days for certified refurbished units 7. And it never defines the word. The term with no stated endpoint is the one Corsair applies to every module it sells.

ExpertFor specialists

Two of these definitions turn on the same twelve-word phrase, which tells you something about where the language comes from.

Kingston: "Product lifetime is defined as the normal time expectancy for the use of products in the industry." 1 Patriot: "The warranty is valid for the life of the Product, which is defined as the normal time expectancy for the use of products in the industry. It does not refer to the lifetime of the original purchaser." 2 “The normal time expectancy for the use of products in the industry” is shared boilerplate rather than two companies independently arriving at the same phrase, and Patriot’s added sentence is the plainest statement of the point any of the six makes.

But Patriot publishes a second, different definition of the same term. Its support FAQ says: "Limited Lifetime offers product warranty for as long as the product is produced and manufactured by Patriot Memory, Inc." with the consequence that "If your product is no longer in production, it will be substituted with a product of similar function and market value." 3 Those are not the same promise. An industry-normal service expectancy and Patriot’s own production run can differ by years in either direction, and nothing on either page reconciles them or says which governs. Both were live at the date of this check.

One near-contradiction that turns out not to be one, recorded because it is easy to trip over. Kingston runs a second warranty page granting only "one (1) year from the date of shipment" 9. That page is not a contradiction of the lifetime term: it states its own scope, covering "DRAM component products" where "the OEM customer (‘Customer’)" buys under "the business to business commercial contract" 9. It governs chips sold to system builders, not the module in a retail box. A reader who lands on it from a search for “Kingston DRAM warranty” will nonetheless see one year and no cue that it is not theirs.

And one brand whose warranty is now a promise about the past. Micron announced its exit from the Crucial consumer business on 3 December 2025 and committed to "continued warranty service and support for Crucial products" 10 — so Crucial cover survives the brand’s withdrawal from retail. Crucial’s own site could not be retrieved for this check, so nothing here describes its terms.

2.Whose lifetime is it?

SimpleStart here

Strip away the wording and there are really four different clocks running, and which one you are on decides everything.

  1. An industry norm. Kingston and Patriot’s warranty page: however long the industry considers normal for the part 1 2.
  2. How long it stays on sale. PNY: cover ends when the item is "no longer available on the market as a new product" 4.
  3. How long the company keeps making it. Patriot’s support FAQ: "for as long as the product is produced and manufactured by Patriot Memory, Inc." 3.
  4. Whatever the company decides later. Silicon Power: "(The end of product life cycle is determined by SP)" 5.

The practical difference: on clock 1 a discontinued kit may still be covered. On clocks 2 and 3, discontinuation is exactly what ends it.

DeeperThe detail

Clocks 2 and 3 are the ones that catch people, because they run against the buyer over time in a way the marketing never suggests. The longer you own the part, the more likely it is to have left the market — so the cover thins precisely as the hardware ages into the years you might actually need it.

Patriot at least publishes what happens next: "If your product is no longer in production, it will be substituted with a product of similar function and market value." 3 So the promise does not vanish; it converts into a substitution at Patriot’s valuation. Corsair makes the same move in different words: "An obsolete or discontinued product will be replaced by the same product if available. If unavailable, Corsair will replace it with a product of similar function and capacity." 7 Kingston’s version adds a third option and puts a figure on it: "In the event a product has been discontinued, Kingston, at its sole discretion, shall either repair the product, offer to replace it with a comparable product or provide a refund at the lesser of the purchase price or the product’s current value." 11

Read Kingston’s carefully. "the lesser of the purchase price or the product’s current value" means that on a part whose price has fallen, you are refunded the lower, current figure — and on a part whose price has risen, which memory has done sharply through 2026, you are refunded what you originally paid. The clause is written so the buyer cannot gain either way.

PNY, on the current revision of its document, says least about substitution of any of them — see the Expert level, because the reason is a change PNY made to its own text.

ExpertFor specialists

PNY has two warranty revisions live on its own site simultaneously, and the newer one is quieter about what you get back.

RevisionPNY’s obligation, as printed
18 January 2016"PNY’s sole obligation under this warranty is to replace or, at its option, to repair with new or remanufactured product, free of charge, all the product’s defective parts." 12
Rev. 2025 (file dated 4-16-25)"PNY’s sole obligation under this warranty is to replace or, at its option, to repair, free of charge, all the product’s defective parts." 4

The 2025 revision deletes the words "with new or remanufactured product". So PNY’s current documentation no longer states what condition a replacement arrives in. This page does not claim the deletion was intended to change the obligation — PNY publishes no explanation, and none was found. It records that the words were there in 2016 and are not there now, and that both documents remain retrievable.

A third PNY formulation exists in its European return conditions: "Regarding RMA for exchange, PNY’s commitment is to return to the customer a similar or superior product." 13 That is a better promise than either US revision, and it applies to a different set of buyers.

For contrast, the two brands that say plainly what you get. Corsair puts a date on it: "If you have owned the product for over 60 days, Corsair will send a refurbished replacement. Refurbished replacements carry a warranty equal to the remaining time on the original purchase’s warranty or 30 days, whichever is higher." 7 Sixty days is the cliff, and Corsair is the only one of the six to publish such a threshold. Silicon Power is blunter still: "SP will either replace it with a refurbished、new one or repair it to be functionally equivalent to a new one. Hence, the repaired product that you receive might not be the one you originally sent." 5 The CJK comma inside the English sentence is as printed.

Kingston: "Repaired parts or replacement products will be provided by Kingston on an exchange basis, and will be either new or recertified." 11 Patriot says only that it "will repair or replace the defective product(s) using equivalent components made by various vendors" 3, that "Repaired or replacement products may be provided in bulk or non-retail packaging (‘bare drive/module’)." 2, and that "Refunds will not be provided for any product." 3 Lexar offers "repair, replace, or refund" at its option and, alone with Kingston, states what a refund is worth: "A refund would be the purchase price less any rebates or the current market value of the Product at the time the warranty claim is made to Lexar, whichever is lower." 6 — the same lesser-of structure Kingston uses.

3.The part you cannot check

SimpleStart here

Here is the gap that follows from all of the above, and it is the most consequential thing on this page.

Four of the six tie your cover to a fact about the catalogue you cannot look up — and a fifth ties it to a decision only that company makes. None of them publishes a way to find out where you stand.

PNY’s cover ends when the item is "no longer available on the market as a new product" 4. Patriot’s FAQ version lasts "for as long as the product is produced and manufactured by Patriot Memory, Inc." — after which it converts to substitution rather than lapsing 3. Kingston’s runs to "the normal time expectancy for the use of products in the industry" 1. And Silicon Power’s runs "until the end of product life cycle. (The end of product life cycle is determined by SP)" 5.

So in five cases out of six, the answer to “am I still covered?” turns on something the company does not publish in a form you can check. Corsair is the exception, and only because it defines nothing at all.

DeeperThe detail

This was checked rather than assumed. For each of the four, the search was for any published end-of-life list, discontinued-product index or notice mechanism a consumer could consult:

BrandResult
PNYNo list found on either warranty revision or its returns pages 4 12
PatriotAn "EOL Products" lifecycle table on the warranty page — covering SSDs, flash cards, USB drives and accessories, with no DRAM entries 2
KingstonNo consumer-facing index found from its support navigation 11
Silicon PowerNo list, no notice mechanism and no definition of "product life cycle" found on its warranty page 5

One qualification, because it matters. Kingston does issue end-of-life and product-change notices, as individual PDFs aimed at channel partners rather than at the person who bought one kit. No browsable index of them was found from Kingston’s support navigation in this check 11, so this is reported as “not found from the consumer path” rather than as an absence.

Two brands do publish discontinued-product lists, and in both cases it is the wrong catalogue. Patriot’s warranty page carries a "Product Lifecycle Table" with repeated "EOL Products" sections and per-model entries 2; Lexar’s carries sections headed "Other Jumpdrive Flash Drives (Discontinued)" and "Platinum, Platinum ll, Premium Series (Discontinued)", each with its warranty period — the lower-case "ll" is as printed 6. Both are flash, SSD and memory-card lists. Neither has DRAM entries, so neither answers the question this section asks.

ExpertFor specialists

Put the two halves together and the shape of the problem is clear. The brands whose definitions create the need for a list do not publish one; the brand that publishes one does not need it.

This page is deliberately not claiming bad faith. A discontinued-product register is a real maintenance burden, and none of these companies is under an obligation this page can point to that requires one. What it does claim is narrower and harder to argue with: a term whose endpoint the buyer cannot determine is not a term the buyer can rely on, and five of six memory warranties are written that way.

Silicon Power’s is the extreme case, and it is worth separating from the other three. The others turn on a fact about the world — is this part still made, still sold, still normal for the industry — that is at least in principle checkable, even if nobody publishes it. Silicon Power’s turns on a decision: "(The end of product life cycle is determined by SP)" 5. No notice mechanism, no discontinued list and no definition of “product life cycle” was found on its warranty page 5. There is nothing to check even in principle.

And one brand escapes the whole problem by not making the claim. Corsair grants lifetime cover to every DRAM module it sells and defines nothing 7 — which cuts both ways. There is no published time limit you can fall foul of, and none you can hold Corsair to. It does publish an endpoint of a different kind: cover "is limited to the original purchaser and is non-transferable, except where prohibited by applicable local law" 7.

4.What quietly voids it

SimpleStart here

The clauses that actually cost people their cover are mundane. Three are worth knowing before you buy.

1. Taking the heat spreader off. Patriot voids cover for a module "missing, damaged, detached, or altered serial number labels, warranty stickers, or heat spreaders" 2, and says it again in its FAQ 3. If you have read our guide to whose DRAM chips are inside your memory stick, note that the method it describes — looking at the chips — ends a Patriot warranty.

2. Where you bought it. All six restrict cover to authorised channels. PNY "will not warranty items that are purchased second-hand" 4; Silicon Power excludes "Products purchased through unauthorized distribution channels or secondhand resale markets" 5.

3. Throwing the box away. PNY requires "proof of purchase documentation evidencing the date of purchase (sales receipt or invoice) and original packaging" 4. It is the only one of the six to demand the packaging, and it is a lifetime warranty.

DeeperThe detail

Taking those in order, with the exact wording, because the differences in scope are large.

The heat spreader. Patriot is the only brand of the six that names it. Its warranty page lists under a heading it flags itself as important: "Label Integrity (Important): Product missing, damaged, detached, or altered serial number labels, warranty stickers, or heat spreaders." 2 Its FAQ repeats the substance 3. Silicon Power reaches a similar result without using the word, excluding cover "When the warranty seal or anti-tamper label is altered or damaged" and where "Product labels are unclear, damaged, missing, illegible, or visibly compromised" 5 — on most modules the label and the spreader come away together. The other four rely on generic language: Kingston’s "any unauthorized disassembly, repair or modification" 1, Corsair’s "Any modifications, abuse, accidents, misapplication, or unauthorized repair" 7, Lexar’s "alteration" 6, PNY’s "repaired or altered in any way that adversely affects its performance or reliability" 4.

The channel. Kingston’s version carries a qualifier that must not be dropped: "To the extent permitted by applicable laws, products not imported or sold through authorized Kingston dealers or distributors are not covered by this warranty." 1 That opening clause is Kingston conceding the exclusion may be unenforceable in some jurisdictions. Patriot excludes "Products purchased from unauthorized resellers" 2. Lexar frames it as scope rather than exclusion: cover "applies only to Lexar consumer products purchased by the original end-user purchaser (‘you’) from Lexar or a Lexar authorized seller" 6 — its global PDF carries no equivalent clause 8. And Corsair names DRAM first among the categories it restricts: in the United States its warranty applies to "DRAM, SSD, gaming chairs, headsets, keyboards, mice, mousepads, peripheral accessories, and Elgato-branded products" only if bought "from Corsair.com or a Corsair-authorized reseller" 7.

Why the channel clause matters more in India than in most markets. Parallel imports are common, and several of these brands publish a very short list of authorised Indian channels — so the distance between “a genuine Patriot module” and “a module with a valid Patriot warranty” can be the seller, not the part. The individual brand guides in this section record what each publishes for India.

ExpertFor specialists

The full exclusion lists, for the two brands that publish compact ones.

Kingston: cover excludes damage that results from "(a) improper installation, accident, abuse, misuse, natural disaster, insufficient or excessive electrical supply, abnormal mechanical or environmental conditions, (b) cosmetic damage, including but not limited to scratches, dents and cracks on touch-screens or casings; or (c) any unauthorized disassembly, repair or modification." 1

Corsair: "Wear and tear from normal use"; "Any modifications, abuse, accidents, misapplication, or unauthorized repair"; "Any improper operations, including use not in accordance with supplied product instructions"; "Connection to any improper voltage supplies"; "Any other cause not related to a product defect in materials or workmanship"; and, distinctively, "Products damaged during crypto mining operations" 7.

Note what is absent from Corsair’s list, since it bears on the next section: the words overclock, heat spreader and tamper were searched for and none appears; disassembly appears once, in a note discouraging disassembly of Corsair’s Hydro X coolers, not as a memory exclusion 7. The only hook a modification would hang on is the generic "Any modifications".

Two further exclusions worth isolating.

Data. Lexar states that it "does not warrant against loss of any data or images (including data stored on Product returned to Lexar for service)" 6. That is ordinary for storage, and on a DRAM module it is close to meaningless — memory does not retain data with the power off — which is a reminder that several of these documents are written to cover a whole catalogue rather than the part you bought.

Statutory rights. Five of the six acknowledge that law sits above their terms, though only two name a jurisdiction outside the United States, and only one quantifies it. Kingston, in capitals as printed: "WITH REGARDS TO CITIZENS OF THE UK OR COUNTRIES THAT ARE MEMBERS OF THE EUROPEAN UNION, IF THIS PRODUCT IS PURCHASED BY A CONSUMER AND NOT IN THE COURSE OF A BUSINESS, THIS WARRANTY IS IN ADDITION TO YOUR LEGAL RIGHTS IN RELATION TO PRODUCTS WHICH ARE DEFECTIVE." 1 Corsair puts a number on it: "In the European Union, the minimum warranty period provided by resellers is 2 years." 7 PNY’s document has no EU or UK statutory statement at all on the pages read — only a US state-law sentence 4 — and Silicon Power’s only EU reference attaches to power banks, not memory 5.

5.Does turning on XMP void it?

SimpleStart here

A widespread belief, and it is worth settling: does switching on XMP or EXPO void your memory warranty?

On the evidence of what these six brands publish, no. Not one of them conditions its warranty on leaving the profile off. Their warranty documents were read for the words overclock, XMP and EXPO, and the only brand whose warranty mentions overclocking at all is Patriot — whose clause, read in full, points the other way.

The belief is real, but it belongs somewhere else. Intel and AMD say it about their processors — and both, read in full, turn on the same hinge the module brands do. Intel calls XMP "a type of memory overclocking" and says that "using it beyond the given specifications may void the processor warranty" 14. AMD’s wording is broader but carries the same condition: overclocking "to operate outside of AMD’s published specifications will void any applicable AMD product warranty, even when enabled via AMD hardware and/or software" 15. A rated profile is a published specification. Those clauses govern the chip you put the memory next to, and they bite when you go past what is published — not when you use it.

DeeperThe detail

Corsair does not merely permit the profile — it ties its own performance guarantee to it: "When you purchase a memory kit from CORSAIR, the listed speed of our memory modules is the maximum speed that the modules are guaranteed to run at when using XMP." 16 Read that against a warranty that would end if you used XMP and the two cannot both stand. Corsair’s do not conflict: the word overclock does not appear in its warranty exclusions at all 7.

Patriot is the only one of the six with overclocking language inside the warranty itself, and the qualifier is the whole point. It excludes damage from "Insufficient or excessive electrical supply (including damage caused by overclocking or overvolting beyond published specifications)" 2. The profile is the published specification — the same company’s product page invites you to use it: "Instantly unlock performance with XMP 3.0 and EXPO support! Kits include pre-tested profiles for zero-hassle setup—simply select the profile in your BIOS to activate enhanced speed." 17 So the clause excludes going past the rated profile, not using it.

Kingston comes closest to a warning and stops short of the word. Its overclocking page says: "While generally safe and supported by Intel, AMD, and the motherboard/system manufacturers, overclocking is done at your own risk. System stability may be compromised by some speeds and configurations." 18 “At your own risk” is not “voids your warranty”, and the distinction is one readers routinely collapse. The word warranty appears on that page only in its navigation.

Kingston also states the mechanism plainly, which explains why none of this is exotic: "Most Kingston FURY/HyperX memory use XMP/EXPO profiles to overclock. These modules ship from the factory at JEDEC standard values." and "The XMP/EXPO profile can then be enabled within the system BIOS." 19 On most parts the rated speed only exists via the profile — the subject of our XMP and EXPO explainer. Kingston names its own exception: its Plug-N-Play FURY Beast and Impact modules "ship from the factory with the advertised overclock speed and latency programmed as the default values" 19.

ExpertFor specialists

What was actually checked, so the negative can be relied on. Each brand’s warranty document was read, plus its own overclocking or profile page where one exists: Kingston 1 18 19, Corsair 7 16 20, Patriot 2 3 17, Lexar 6 8 21, PNY 4 22, Silicon Power 5 23. No clause conditioning warranty cover on not enabling XMP or EXPO was found in any of them. Lexar’s own overclocking explainer mentions warranty only in its footer navigation 21, and describes XMP profiles as "essentially factory-tested overclocks" that are "carefully validated configurations that we guarantee will work", and Silicon Power’s EXPO definition carries no risk language of any kind 23.

The limit of that finding, stated plainly. This is an absence established across the documents named above. It is not a promise that no brand will ever decline a claim on stability grounds, and it says nothing about manual overclocking past the rated profile, which is a different act. Corsair addresses that case and still does not reach for the warranty — it disclaims liability instead: "Corsair, inc. is not liable for any damages incurred by following the steps provided in the article below." 20 The lower-case "inc." is as printed.

The tension people expect is narrower than it looks. AMD’s clause is broad enough to reach the memory as well as the processor — it names "AMD processors and memory", voids "any applicable AMD product warranty", and adds that overclocking "may also void warranties offered by the system manufacturer or retailer" 15. But its trigger is operating "outside of AMD’s published specifications", and an EXPO profile is one of AMD’s published specifications. So enabling EXPO sits inside the condition rather than outside it, on AMD’s own wording. Our XMP and EXPO explainer sets out both companies’ wording in full, including the places where they differ.

One labelling error worth recording because it muddies exactly this question. PNY’s XLR8 DDR5-6400 sell sheet prints the frequency under the heading "Frequency Speed (JEDEC) 6400MHz (PC5-51200)" while the same sheet advertises "Intel XMP & AMD EXPO Support" 22. DDR5-6400 is not a JEDEC speed a module boots at; labelling a profile speed “JEDEC” leaves a buyer with no way to tell from the sheet what the part is supposed to run at unaided.

6.Why they want the whole kit back

SimpleStart here

Memory is sold in matched kits. If one stick of a pair fails, do you send back one or both?

Three of the six say both. Three say nothing at all — which is not the same as saying one is fine.

BrandWhole kit required?
KingstonYes 11
PatriotYes, with an exception you must ask for 24
CorsairYes — but not in the warranty 25
LexarNo statement found 6
PNYNo statement found 4
Silicon PowerNo statement found 5

The reason is the same one that makes buying a second stick later a gamble: a replacement has to match what is beside it, and the brand can only guarantee that within a kit it packed.

DeeperThe detail

In their own words. Kingston: "Products purchased as part of a kit require that the kit be returned in its entirety in order to be eligible for warranty." 11 Patriot: "All DRAM memory module kit(s) must be returned in its entirety." 3 — the number disagreement is Patriot’s — and its FAQ gives the reason and the escape hatch together: "We require the return of entire kits for RMA (Return Merchandise Authorization) submissions. This policy is in place to guarantee the compatibility of replacement modules and facilitate a seamless transition." followed by "Should you need to deviate from this requirement, please contact us prior to shipment. The RMA department will evaluate such requests individually." 24

Corsair’s rule is real but is not where a buyer would look for it. The sentence — "If memory modules were purchased as a kit, then the entire kit must be returned for warranty replacement, even if not all the modules are faulty." — sits in a returns help article 25, not in the Limited Warranty 7. Someone who reads the warranty document end to end will not find it.

Patriot is the only one of the three to publish a way out, and it is worth reading precisely: the deviation must be requested before shipping, and Patriot commits only to evaluating it "individually" 24. That is a discretion, not a right.

For the three that say nothing — Lexar, PNY and Silicon Power — the honest position is that their warranty documents were read and no kit rule was found 6 4 5. It does not follow that a single stick will be accepted; it follows that the published terms do not address it.

ExpertFor specialists

The kit rule is the warranty consequence of a fact set out on our whose dies are inside page: the chips in a part number can change without the part number changing, and both Corsair and Kingston say so in writing. A brand that replaces one stick of a pair from current stock cannot guarantee the new module holds the same silicon as the survivor — so it takes the pair.

Which makes the three silences more awkward than they first appear. A brand can only be sure a replacement matches if it replaces the set, and a brand that publishes no kit rule has not told the buyer which way it will resolve that. This page does not treat the silence as permission, and a buyer should not either.

One practical consequence follows for the purchase itself rather than the claim, and it is the single most useful thing to take from this page. Buy the capacity you want in one kit, in one box. Patriot is explicit about mixing 17; Kingston permits it for JEDEC-spec modules but advises against it on overclocked parts 19. No brand publishes a rule on how two separately bought kits are claimed, so what follows is this page’s inference rather than a published term: on the three brands that require whole-kit returns, each kit is its own return with its own proof of purchase.

Patriot goes one step further than a kit rule and disclaims the performance itself on mixed singles: "Patriot will not guarantee products meet advertised specifications when a single module is paired with another single module and operated as a dual kit." 17 So on Patriot, two single modules bought separately are neither a guaranteed-performance configuration nor a straightforward warranty return.

7.What actually happens when you claim

SimpleStart here

What you have to produce, and who pays the postage.

BrandWhat it asks for
Kingston"You will be required to provide proof of purchase." 1
CorsairA photo of the serial number and the receipt 7
LexarProof of purchase and an RMA number 6
PatriotA valid purchase invoice, given to the authorised reseller, and an RMA number visible on the outside of the package 2
PNYReceipt or invoice and original packaging 4
Silicon PowerAny one of: warranty certificate, proof of purchase, or registration details 5

PNY is the strictest and Silicon Power the most forgiving — the only brand of the six that offers alternatives to a receipt, and the only one that accepts product registration on its own.

DeeperThe detail

Shipping is the cost nobody advertises. Kingston splits it cleanly: inbound, "You are responsible for the cost of insuring and shipping your returned products"; outbound, "Repaired or replacement product will be shipped at Kingston’s expense." 1 Patriot does the same and adds a legal hedge: the customer pays to send it, "Patriot will cover the cost of shipping the replacement product back to the customer (standard shipping)", and it "will comply with local regulations regarding shipping costs in jurisdictions where required by law (e.g., certain EU countries for confirmed warranty claims)" 2.

PNY does not split it. Both revisions say: "The consumer is responsible for all shipping costs. When shipping from outside the United States this will include freight, duty, customs fees and taxes." 4 12 On a lifetime warranty claimed from outside the US, the duty and customs on the replacement are the buyer’s.

Corsair’s own pages do not agree on who pays. Its returns article says "You may be required to pay shipping and handling charges, as well as any applicable tariffs, duties, taxes, or other fees" 25; a separate article says "For all authorized RMAs, we provide a prepaid shipping label" 26; and its warranty adds that a buyer outside the product’s market is "responsible for the cost of international return shipping to the nearest Corsair warehouse, as well as shipping of the replacement to you" 7. The "may" leaves room for all three, and the warranty does state its own trigger — a product "not sold in the country to which you moved it". So these are not strictly contradictory; the problem is that no one page tells a buyer which of the three they are in.

Silicon Power states no allocation at all. The only shipping sentence found is a disclaimer: "Silicon Power is not responsible for any product damage or loss resulting from improper shipping or packaging during transit to our service center." 5 Lexar likewise requires that returns be "shipped prepaid and packaged appropriately" and warns it "is not responsible for any Product that is lost or damaged in transit" 6, with its lifetime definition carrying "(shipping costs not included)" 6.

ExpertFor specialists

Only one brand publishes a turnaround commitment. Kingston: "Subject to availability, the replacement part will be shipped to the customer on the following business day of the product being received. All requests are processed within 2 business days." 27 — a commitment with a stock condition on it. It also imposes a deadline the other way — after an RMA number is issued you have "within thirty (30) days" to send the product 1. No published turnaround time was found for Corsair, Patriot, PNY, Lexar or Silicon Power on the pages read.

Two brands publish a choice of process. Corsair: a Standard RMA ships the replacement after the faulty unit is returned, an Advanced RMA ships it before and "requires a credit card" to which "an amount equal to the value of the replacement product is first charged"; Corsair refunds that amount "after the defective product has arrived at Corsair and undergone inspection" 28. Kingston offers the same pair: its advanced replacement also requires a credit card, places a hold for the list price, and obliges the customer to return the defective part "within 10 calendar days after receiving the replacement" 27.

PNY’s stated basis for cover is something PNY says its memory does not have. Its knowledge base answers the registration question: "No. Warranty coverage is based on the product’s serial number and valid proof of purchase from an authorized reseller. Registration is optional." 29 A separate article states that its RAM modules "are not assigned unique serial numbers", because "These products are manufactured in very high volumes and tracked at the batch level rather than individually serialized." 30 So coverage is said to rest on a serial number the same company says the product does not carry. This page reports both statements and does not attempt to reconcile them; PNY publishes nothing that does.

Two further mechanics worth having. Transferability: Kingston "This limited warranty is non-transferable" 1; Corsair "limited to the original purchaser and is non-transferable, except where prohibited by applicable local law" 7; Patriot "This warranty is not transferable" 2; PNY, in the 2025 revision, "This warranty is not assignable, and are valid only for the original purchaser of the product" — the number disagreement is PNY’s 4. Lexar and Silicon Power publish no explicit transferability clause on the pages read; Lexar scopes cover to "the original end-user purchaser" without using the word 6, and Silicon Power addresses resale only through its channel exclusion 5. Silence is not transferability, and this page does not read it as such.

A dangling reference. Lexar’s warranty requires compliance with "Lexar’s standard RMA Terms and Conditions which can be found online at www.lexar.com/support" 6. That document was not found at that location in this check. A buyer is bound by terms the page points at and does not deliver.

8.The same kit, different promises by continent

SimpleStart here

The same kit can carry a different promise depending on where you bought it.

PNY is the clearest case: its product pages say Limited Lifetime, and its EMEA document grants "PC Memory : 10 years" — the spacing around the colon is as printed 31. A buyer in Europe reading the product page sees a lifetime; the document that governs the purchase says ten years.

Lexar caps it too: "For products purchased in Germany or regions where the limited lifetime warranty is not applicable, the duration of the limited lifetime warranty shall be 10 years." 6

Corsair moves in the opposite direction: its certified refurbished units get ninety days, except that "In the EU, Switzerland, and the United Kingdom, the warranty period is two years by statute" 7 — a warranty table with the law written into a row, turning ninety days into twenty-four months.

DeeperThe detail

Lexar’s two documents are not about the same products, which is easy to miss. Its Americas warranty page caps lifetime cover in "Germany or regions where the limited lifetime warranty is not applicable" 6 — an open category that could reach many jurisdictions. Its global warranty PDF carries a narrower Germany-only note, "limited to 10 years from purchase in Germany", but that note sits on a row covering memory cards and USB flash drives, and the PDF has no row for DRAM at all 8. So the Americas page is the only Lexar document that speaks to a memory module’s term, and it is the open-ended one. Neither document explains why Germany is different, and this page does not supply a reason, because no brand publishes one.

Where you actually send the module varies as much as the term. Kingston publishes real postal addresses on the warranty page itself — Fountain Valley in California, Sunbury on Thames in the UK for Europe, the Middle East and Africa, and Chunan in Taiwan 1. Corsair publishes none on any of its warranty or returns pages, handling regions by rule instead: a buyer outside the product’s market pays "the cost of international return shipping to the nearest Corsair warehouse", and even inside it Corsair commits only to a "best effort" to arrange an in-country replacement 7. PNY documents Europe and nothing else: its European procedure requires a completed RMA form, the number on the outside of the box and proof of purchase inside, with products "shipped to PNY Mérignac" — and gives no street address, only the city 13.

Patriot publishes one explicit regional cap of its own: "For Russia, a lifetime warranty is defined as the period between ten (10) years from the original date of purchase." 3 The phrasing is as printed.

For India specifically, Kingston publishes a phone number and no Indian return address, so the nearest published destination is the UK 32. Silicon Power’s warranty page gives a Taiwan service-centre address and names no Indian route 5; whether one exists elsewhere on its support site could not be established here. The individual brand guides in this section record what each publishes.

ExpertFor specialists

Patriot handles India in a way no other brand in this section does, and it is the most unusual clause found in this check.

For Amazon purchases "in certain regions, such as India, where direct product returns to Patriot Memory facilities present shipping difficulties", Patriot asks for "Photographs of the product.", "Proof of purchase (e.g., Amazon order confirmation or invoice).", and — the striking one — "Proof of product destruction." It then states: "Once we receive and verify this documentation, Patriot Memory will process a refund for your purchase through Amazon." 33

That sits awkwardly against Patriot’s warranty FAQ, which states: "Patriot warranty service is only for the replacement of defective Patriot products. Refunds will not be provided for any product." 3 Note what this is and is not. It is FAQ against FAQ, two articles on the same support host — Patriot’s current warranty document says nothing about refunds either way 2. And the Amazon provision is expressly scoped to one sales channel and to regions where returns "present shipping difficulties". So the likeliest reading is a general rule and a narrow exception rather than a conflict — but Patriot publishes nothing that says so, and a buyer reading the flat sentence would not know the exception existed.

The destruction requirement is worth pausing on rather than treating as an oddity. It is a rational answer to a real problem — cross-border RMA shipping on a low-value item can cost more than the item — and it converts the warranty from a replacement into a refund. But it also means an Indian buyer’s remedy requires destroying working-enough hardware and documenting it, and it applies only to one sales channel. A buyer who bought the same module from an Indian retailer rather than Amazon is not covered by this article at all, and Patriot’s warranty documents carry no regional RMA addresses; its FAQ says only "Consumers should contact the Patriot location nearest them for RMA support. For a list of locations, please visit our ‘Contact Us’ link." 3 — a list this check did not follow, so no claim is made about what it contains

The general shape, then. The advertised term is global; the document that governs it is regional; and the route to a claim is the most regional thing of all. Of the six, only Kingston publishes addresses a buyer can actually post to on more than one continent 1. If you are buying outside the United States, the term on the product page is the least reliable part of the promise — find the document for your region before you rely on it.
