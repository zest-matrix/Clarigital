Silicon Power: a storage brand that sells memory, listed on TPEx

Silicon Power is a Taipei company, listed on the Taipei Exchange since 2012, that calls itself a storage brand and sells DRAM as one of seven product lines. It is one of the very few brands in this section that publishes a factory address. It is also Its warranty leaves the end of "lifetime" to its own discretion, and whose own About page claims a subsidiary its own locations page does not list. 1.At a glance

SimpleStart here

Silicon Power is a Taipei company, founded in February 2003, that sells memory cards, USB drives, portable drives, SSDs, memory modules and industrial memory 1.

Note the order it puts those in. Its own words: "We are a leading manufacturer of memory cards, USB drives, external hard drives, SSDs, memory modules, and industrial memory products." 1 Memory modules come fifth of six. Its site title describes the company as "A leading brand in high-quality, personalized, and unique storage solutions" 2 — storage, not memory.

Two things make it worth a full guide anyway. It is publicly listed, which means real filings exist 3. And it is one of very few brands in this section that publishes a factory address 4.

DeeperThe detail

The listing is the most useful fact here, and it needs stating precisely because the detail is easy to get wrong. Silicon Power trades on the Taipei Exchange under code 4973, listed on 19 June 2012, classified in the Semiconductor Industry, ISIN TW0004973003 3.

It is not on the TWSE main board. The exchange’s own ISIN record gives the market type as "TPEx LISTED" 3, and a main-board daily-trading query for 4973 returns no records at all 5. This site made exactly that main-board-versus-TPEx error about another Taiwanese company in an earlier session, so it is checked and stated explicitly here.

From the Taiwan government company registry, the corporate skeleton 6:

Chinese name廣穎電通股份有限公司
Registered address臺北市內湖區洲子街106號7樓
Authorised capitalNT$1,000,000,000
Paid-in capitalNT$652,928,430
Incorporated18 February 2003
Status核准設立 (approved and established)

The registry address matches the address Silicon Power publishes itself — "7F., No. 106, Zhouzi St., Neihu District, Taipei City 114, Taiwan" 4 — and the registry’s incorporation date matches its own "established in February 2003" 1 6. No page was found giving a different founding year.

ExpertFor specialists

Being listed ought to mean the numbers are available. In practice they are not, and the reason is worth recording because it is a choice Silicon Power has made about its own disclosure.

Every financial document on Silicon Power’s investor pages is hosted on Google Drive, and none of them could be retrieved for this check 7. Its financial-information page contains no figures at all — only links to 2022, 2023 and 2024 consolidated financial statements as Drive files 7. Its shareholder-services page links the top-ten shareholder list the same way 8. So revenue, segment mix and ownership structure are unestablished here, and this page states no figure for any of them.

That is not a small gap for a listed company. A reader wanting to know what share of Silicon Power’s business DRAM actually is cannot find out from its website, and this guide will not estimate it. What can be said is qualitative and is the company’s own: memory modules are fifth of six in its self-description 1, DRAM is one of seven top-level product categories 2, and of 61 FAQs across the site only 11 are about memory 9 10.

Regulator routes were attempted and did not help. Taiwan’s Market Observation Post System page rendered as an empty script shell, and the Taipei Exchange’s own site refused or returned nothing for this company. None of that is evidence about Silicon Power — it is a limit of this check, and it means the filings a listed company must make exist somewhere this guide could not read.

One structural warning for anyone re-checking the listing. The Chinese-language edition of the exchange’s ISIN page is Big5-encoded and decodes to mojibake through automated retrieval; a first pass through it appeared to resolve the market type as main-board listed, which is wrong. Only the English edition gave a trustworthy value 3. Cite that one.

DeeperThe detail

Where the company exists is where its own two pages stop agreeing, and this is the sharpest contradiction on the site.

The About page says: "Silicon Power is headquartered in Taipei, Taiwan, with subsidiaries in the Netherlands, Japan, the United States, Hong Kong, and India." 1

The Asia locations page lists: Taiwan, Japan, China, India, Vietnam, Philippines, Indonesia. Hong Kong does not appear on it at all 4.

The two lists disagree in both directions. The About page claims a Hong Kong subsidiary the locations page never mentions; the locations pages show offices in China (Wuhan and Shanghai), Vietnam, the Philippines, Indonesia and a German office that the About page omits from its subsidiary list 1 4 12.

And a third disagreement the About page does not notice: it lists "Netherlands 丨 Amsterdam" 1 while the Europe page puts the Netherlands subsidiary at "Antennestraat 16, 1322AB, Almere, The Netherlands" 12 — a different city. The entities that are documented with addresses: a German office at "Waldstraße 21, 86517 Wehringen" 12; and the US subsidiary at "4590 Enterprise Street, Fremont, CA 94538,U.S.A" 13. The missing space after the comma and the full stop-less "U.S.A" are as printed.

The Asia detail is fuller than most brands publish: a Japan subsidiary in Taito, Tokyo, an Osaka office, and Chinese offices in Wuhan and Shanghai with phone numbers 4.

ExpertFor specialists

What none of those pages give is a legal entity name for any subsidiary. The Europe page prints "Europe Subsidiary" and the Americas page prints "US Subsidiary" — job titles rather than registered names 12 13. So the legal identities of Silicon Power’s overseas entities are unestablished here, and the only registered name this page states is the Taiwanese parent, confirmed against the government registry 6.

The one route to those names would be the subsidiary list in an annual report, and Silicon Power’s annual report is a Google Drive file linked among its 2024 shareholder-meeting materials that could not be retrieved 8; its consolidated financial statements are hosted the same way 7. That is a gap, not a finding — the information almost certainly exists in a document this check could not open.

A second thing the registry gives that the website does not: the 負責人, the legally responsible person, recorded as 陳慧民 6. Silicon Power names no executive anywhere on the pages read for this check — no chief executive, no chairman, no management page. Checked the About page, the Chinese About page, the business page and the investor pages, and every headline on the news index, whose six pages were sampled at first and last 1 14 15 11 16. No named officer was found on any of them.

Finally, a quiet oddity in the bilingual material. The Traditional Chinese About page carries the same location list as the English one, using the CJK character 丨 rather than a pipe as separator — both editions do it — "India 丨 Bangalore" 1 14. But the Chinese page carries no ISO certification statement at all, where the English business page does 14 15. The two language editions are not translations of each other.

3.The story

SimpleStart here

Silicon Power was established in February 2003 and describes its purpose in one sentence: "Silicon Power, established in February 2003, focuses on building its own brand and expanding globally." 1

It went public on the Taipei Exchange on 19 June 2012, nine years after founding 3.

The company’s own proudest claim is a quality award, and it is worth quoting exactly as printed because of how it is dated: "With strict quality control, we have won the Taiwan Excellence Award for nine consecutive years as of 2016." 1

That sentence is on a live 2026 page. The 2017 to 2026 record is not mentioned.

DeeperThe detail

There is not much more corporate history published, and the absence is itself worth recording. Silicon Power publishes no founding story, no founder name and no milestone timeline on the pages read here, and names no officer on any of them — the About page, the Chinese About page, the business page, the investor pages and the news-index headlines 1 14 15 11 16.

Compare that with the module brands already covered here: Kingston publishes a decade-by-decade timeline, Corsair files audited accounts naming its controlling shareholder, Patriot at least states a founding year in press releases. Silicon Power, a listed company, publishes less narrative than several private ones.

What the record does show is a company that moved from flash storage into memory rather than the other way round. Its self-description leads with memory cards and USB drives and puts modules fifth 1; its site title calls it a storage brand 2; and its most recent enterprise registered-memory launch is dated March 2026 17.

The brand line, from the About page, is "Memory is Personal" 1 — which for a company whose memory business is one of seven categories reads as aspiration rather than description.

ExpertFor specialists

Two structural facts about the company’s trajectory can be established from its own pages, and both point the same way.

First, the enterprise line is small but not new. Silicon Power describes the DDR5 RDIMM launch of 19 March 2026 as a continuation rather than a debut — it announces "its latest DDR5 RDIMM module" and says the company "continues to expand its enterprise memory portfolio" 17 — and its server-and-workstation category contains exactly two product families, DDR5 RDIMM and DDR4 RDIMM 18. How long it has sold registered memory cannot be established from what it publishes: no launch date for the DDR4 RDIMM appears anywhere on the reachable site.

Second, the gaming push is newer still. Its most recent memory announcement is the ROG-certified XPOWER Cyclone R DDR5 of 26 May 2026 19, and its XPOWER catalogue runs to thirteen families including a DDR5 CUDIMM 20 — the clocked module type that only reached the market with Intel’s Z890 platform.

So the shape is a long-standing consumer memory brand with a small enterprise line it says it is expanding, while refreshing its gaming line: a new RDIMM in March 2026, ROG-certified gaming DDR5 in May 2026. Whether that is a strategic shift or opportunism in a high-priced memory market cannot be established from what Silicon Power publishes, because it publishes no strategy statement and no segment revenue 7. This page records the timing and declines to read intent into it.

One thing worth noting for balance, since this section has been critical of the company’s disclosure: Silicon Power does publish an ISO 9001 and ISO 14001 claim 15, a factory street address 4 and two named third-party host platforms its industrial storage is demonstrated in — AAEON’s BOXER-8645AI and ADLINK’s DLAP-411-Orin, by model number 21. That is a booth demonstration rather than a stated design win, but naming the host platforms at all is more than most brands here do. Several better-known brands in this section publish none of those.

4.The lineup

SimpleStart here

Silicon Power sells 23 DRAM families across four categories, spanning DDR3 to DDR5 22:

CategoryFamilies
XPOWER gaming13 — Cyclone R, Cyclone CUDIMM, Cyclone, Storm, Pulse and Zenith on DDR5; Zenith, Turbine, Turbine RGB and AirCool on DDR4 20 23
Desktop UDIMM4 — DDR5, DDR4, DDR3L and DDR3 24
Laptop SODIMM4 — DDR5, DDR4, DDR3L and DDR3 25
Server and workstation2 — DDR5 RDIMM and DDR4 RDIMM 18

Two things stand out. It still sells DDR3, which most brands here have dropped 24. And it sells a DDR5 CUDIMM — the clocked module with its own timing chip, one of the newest module types there is 20.

DeeperThe detail

The speed and capacity range the catalogue advertises is wide: "1066" to "9200" MT/s and 4GB to 64GB 22.

The RDIMM line is the newest and the most specific. Its DDR5 RDIMM page lists eight parts at 16, 32 and 64GB, 4800 to 6400 MT/s, all at 1.1 V 26. Silicon Power describes the technology in its launch release: the module "integrates advanced On-Die ECC technology alongside a Registering Clock Driver (RCD)", "significantly enhancing fault tolerance compared to standard ECC UDIMMs" 17.

The catalogue paginates, and a reader who stops at the first screen sees less than half of it. The memory index runs to three numbered pages and the XPOWER index to two — its own links read "01 02 03" and "Last Page (3)" 22 20. The pagination is query-string based rather than path based, so a guessed path URL returns 404 while the site’s own links work. Four of the six DDR4 families sit on the second page — only XPOWER Pulse DDR4 and XPOWER Zenith RGB DDR4 appear on the first — which is why the four sub-category pages are the more reliable route 24 25 18 20.

Page two of the XPOWER index is where the older lines live: XPOWER Zenith DDR4, XPOWER Turbine RGB DDR4, XPOWER Turbine DDR4 and XPOWER AirCool DDR4 20. The Turbine RGB page carries nine parts at 3200 and 3600 MT/s, 1.35 V, and a Taiwanese design patent, "TW Patent No. D199314" 23.

ExpertFor specialists

Part numbers are published in quantity and the scheme is clearly systematic. Examples, exactly as Silicon Power prints them: SP032GBLVU560F02 (a 32GB DDR5-5600 UDIMM) 27, SP016GBSFU320X02 (a 16GB DDR4-3200 SODIMM) 28, SP064GBRVE640J02 (a 64GB DDR5-6400 RDIMM) 26, SP008GXLZU320BSB (an XPOWER Turbine RGB DDR4-3200 part) 23.

No decoder is published. Checked the memory FAQ, the full FAQ index, the downloads page, the e-catalogue page and every product page above 10 9 29 30 27 31 28 26. No part-number key was found. A scheme is inferable from the pattern, but that inference is ours and this page does not present it as Silicon Power’s.

The scheme is also not internally consistent in the one field a buyer would most want to read. Within the RDIMM table, 4800 MT/s encodes as 480 and 6400 as 640 — but 5600 encodes as 56A 26. So even pattern-matching fails on the speed digits.

And two parts in the DDR4 RDIMM table differ only by a B02 versus F02 suffix at the same 16GB capacity 32. What that suffix encodes is not published, and this page does not guess — but it is precisely the kind of distinction that would tell a buyer whether two modules are the same part inside.

One more documentation gap. The downloads page lists product eDM files — marketing documents, under a tab labelled "Product eDM" — with version numbers, dates and file sizes — "DDR5 RDIMM | EN | v1.1 | 2026/03 | 1.88 MB" and similar — but exposes metadata only, with no resolvable download links in what rendered for this check 29. The page carries no datasheet or specification section at all, so whether module-level datasheets exist anywhere is unestablished here. Whether they contain rank, organisation or SPD detail is unestablished here. (The inconsistent date formats, 2026/03 against 2026/5, are as printed.)

5.How a module is built

SimpleStart here

Silicon Power buys DRAM chips from the companies that make them and assembles them into modules. It does not make chips.

What it says about the chips is one phrase, and it is the emblematic quote for this whole question. From its enterprise RDIMM launch: "Designed with original high-quality DRAM chips, the new module delivers massive bandwidth and enhanced reliability to support intensive workloads such as artificial intelligence, machine learning, and large-scale data processing." 17

"Original" is the standard Taiwanese-English rendering of 原廠 — chip-vendor-branded silicon rather than remarked or downgraded die. So it is a real sourcing claim. It names nobody.

DeeperThe detail

That absence was checked across everything readable. Pages read and searched for Samsung, SK hynix, Micron, Nanya and CXMT: the memory FAQ 10, the full FAQ index 9, five product pages 27 31 28 26 32, two XPOWER product pages 33 23, the XPOWER brand site 34, the About page 1, the business page 15, the RDIMM release 17 and the downloads page 29. No DRAM manufacturer is named on any of them.

Nor is there any statement that the chips might change. The disclaimers Silicon Power does print are about speed, not components: "Actual speed may vary depending on the system platform and hardware configuration" 27, and on the gaming line "Memory operates at BIOS default speeds by default until XMP or EXPO is activated" 33.

Subject-to-change clauses do exist, and they are not where one might expect. The DDR5 RDIMM page reserves the right to change things: "Specifications and product availability are subject to change without prior notice. Actual performance and system compatibility may vary depending on the server platform architecture and BIOS configuration." 26 The XPOWER gaming pages carry their own version: "Specifications and availability are subject to change without prior notice." 33 23 The pages that carry no such clause are the plain SP UDIMM and SODIMM pages — only a speed caveat 27 31 28 — and the DDR4 RDIMM page, which carries no disclaimer text at all 32. None of the clauses says anything about which company made the DRAM, in either direction.

ExpertFor specialists

What Silicon Power does publish about its process is a quality claim with certifications attached, which is more than several better-known brands here manage. From its business page, all under the heading "100% Commitment to Quality":

"we use a 100% lead-free manufacturing process, complying with EU RoHS, GCC standards, and German Green Dot environmental regulations" — and — "We are also certified with ISO9001 and ISO14001 international standards, and adhere to specifications from associations like SD-3C, LLC, 4C Entity, LLC, and CompactFlash Association, holding all necessary authorizations." 15 The missing spaces in "ISO9001" and "ISO14001" are as printed.

And a component claim, also from that page: "SP insists on using 100% qualified components and ensures all products pass factory and compatibility tests, maintaining the highest quality products and services through rigorous control processes." 15

Neither term is defined. "100% qualified components" states no qualification standard, no supplier list and no acceptance criterion; "factory and compatibility tests" names no protocol. And the ISO claims carry no certificate number, no registrar, no scope statement and no expiry date 15, so a buyer cannot verify either certification independently.

On the gaming line, the testing claims are more specific: "100% testing across major motherboard brands", "QVL certification from leading brands like MSI and GIGABYTE demonstrates Silicon Power’s established market reputation", and "10-layer PCB design for stability" 34. For the enterprise line: modules "rigorously tested to withstand continuous 24/7 server operations", and "undergone extensive compatibility testing with mainstream Intel and AMD server platforms" 17.

But binning is absent everywhere. Checked the XPOWER brand site, the About page, the business page, both FAQ pages, the RDIMM release and six product pages 34 1 15 10 9 17 27 31 28 26 33 23. No statement describing IC binning, sorting, screening or die-grade selection was found on any of them — not even on the enthusiast line, where buyers most expect it.

6.Where it’s made: plants and addresses

SimpleStart here

Here Silicon Power does something rare in this section: it publishes a factory address.

On its Asia locations page, under Taiwan, there is a heading reading simply "Factory", and beneath it 4:

"1F., No. 308-7, Sec. 1, Datong Rd., Xizhi Dist., New Taipei City 221, Taiwan", with phone +886-2-7708-1188.

Most module brands in this section say only which country, or nothing at all. Silicon Power gives a street number.

Its press-release boilerplate refers to the same thing without locating it: "Headquartered in Taipei with a state-of-the-art production site, SP has become a leading manufacturer of flash memory cards, USB flash drives, portable hard drives, solid state drives, DRAM modules, and industrial-grade solutions." 17 The locations page supplies the address the boilerplate withholds.

DeeperThe detail

That address is corroborated from a second, independent page, which is worth more than a single statement. The warranty page gives the RMA department’s address as "Silicon Power RMA Department 5F, No. 308-10, Section 1, Datong Road Xizhi District, New Taipei City 221 Taiwan" 35.

Same street, same block: Datong Road Section 1, numbers 308-7 and 308-10, Xizhi District, New Taipei City. So the Xizhi site functions as both the production facility and the returns department, evidenced by two pages that were written by different parts of the company — the formatting even differs between them ("Sec. 1" and "Datong Rd." against "Section 1" and "Datong Road") 4 35.

There is also a Taiwan service centre listed separately, at +886-2-8751-5326 4.

Two caveats on how far this takes us. The boilerplate says "a state-of-the-art production site" — singular 17 — and only the one Taiwanese address is published, so nothing here establishes whether modules for all markets are assembled there. And the About page names no factory, plant or assembly location at all 1, so the disclosure sits only on the locations page.

ExpertFor specialists

What is not published is capacity, output, headcount or process. No wafer-starts figure, no module output, no line count, no employee number for the Xizhi site — checked the locations page, the About page, the business page, the investor pages and the news index 4 1 15 11 16.

That is unremarkable for a module assembler — there are no wafers to count — but it means the published record establishes that a factory exists and where, and nothing about its scale.

Silicon Power claims no wafer fab on any page read here, describing only "a state-of-the-art production site" 17 with one published Taiwanese address 4. It buys DRAM die and NAND from the companies that do, which is the normal arrangement for every brand in the module half of this section.

The industrial arm is where a fuller manufacturing and component-control story would sit, and this check could not read a single page of it. Silicon Power routes its industrial business to a separate domain, SP Industrial, linked from its own navigation 2 1. Every attempt to retrieve that domain failed at the same point: the fetch could not obtain or parse the host’s robots file, so no path on the host could be retrieved. The cause could not be established here — the failure is at the robots stage, before any page is requested, and it occurred inside this check’s own network 36.

Read that precisely, because the distinction matters. This is not Silicon Power blocking access, and it is not a dead site: the host resolves, sits behind a content-delivery network. What Silicon Power’s server presents to an ordinary browser was not established, because this environment’s own network sits between the two and the failure occurred on this side of it. This page therefore makes no claim that the site is misconfigured, and none that a reader will have trouble reaching it. So everything SP Industrial publishes about wide-temperature parts, long-term availability and bill-of-materials control is unread here. This page therefore makes no claim about what Silicon Power promises industrial customers — neither that it makes such promises nor that it does not. The incomplete chain is itself worth noting as a defect on the company’s own site.

Silicon Power’s own navigation routes industrial material to that separate host 2 1. Whether it previously sat on the main domain is not established here. This page states the retrieval failure as the reason the material is unread, and makes no claim about what any retired pages contained.

7.How the memory works

SimpleStart here

The technology on a Silicon Power module is the same technology every brand here uses: DRAM chips bought from a chip maker — Silicon Power does not say which — a board, and on DDR5 a power-management chip and an SPD hub that describes the module to your computer.

Silicon Power publishes unusually little about this. Its plain consumer pages give capacity, speed, timing, voltage, form factor and a certification list, and stop there — the colour column on those pages is present but empty, every cell reading "n/a" 27 31. A colour value appears only on the XPOWER pages 33.

The one place it explains a technology is the enterprise line, where it is genuinely informative: the DDR5 RDIMM "integrates advanced On-Die ECC technology alongside a Registering Clock Driver (RCD)", "significantly enhancing fault tolerance compared to standard ECC UDIMMs" 17.

DeeperThe detail

That RCD sentence is the most technically useful thing on the site, so it is worth unpacking. A registered module puts a buffer between the memory controller and the DRAM chips, which is what lets a server address far more memory than a desktop can. On-die ECC is separate and universal to DDR5 — every DDR5 chip corrects some of its own errors. Silicon Power is right that the two together give better fault tolerance than an ECC UDIMM, and it is unusual among the brands covered here in explaining that on a product announcement 17.

On the consumer side the gaps are real. No operating or storage temperature range appears on any consumer memory page read for this check — confirmed absent on the DDR4 UDIMM and DDR4 SODIMM pages 31 28. No rank, no chip organisation, no secondary timings, and no SPD or PMIC vendor is named on any of them 27 31 28.

On overclocking profiles, the gaming line at least states the mechanism plainly, which several brands do not: "Memory operates at BIOS default speeds by default until XMP or EXPO is activated." 33 That is the correct description of what a profile is and why a new kit boots slow — the explainer in this section covers the rest.

ExpertFor specialists

One specification row on the DDR4 RDIMM page is, on its face, misleading, and it is Silicon Power’s own labelling rather than a reading error.

The page prints a row labelled "Operating Temp. (Wide)" whose value is "0~85°C" 32. 0 to 85 °C is the ordinary commercial range. A wide-temperature or extended-temperature part means roughly −40 °C at the low end; that is the whole point of the term, and it is what an industrial buyer is paying for. Labelling a commercial range "(Wide)" is misleading as printed, and no footnote or disclaimer is attached to the table 32.

The same table gives "Warranty | Limited Lifetime Warranty", "Tested Speed | 3200MT/s", "Voltage | 1.2V" and "Data Width | 72 Bits" 32 — the 72-bit width being the ECC giveaway, eight bits of parity on top of a 64-bit word.

A smaller typographic habit worth flagging, because it recurs and because this site reproduces source errors rather than tidying them: Silicon Power uses the CJK enumeration comma 、 inside English text. Its DDR4 RDIMM page prints chip density as "1Gx8 (8GB、16GB) /2Gx8 (16GB、32GB)" 32, and its warranty page prints "refurbished、new one" 35. Both are as printed.

Finally, the compatibility tool. Silicon Power runs one on its support subdomain, and it could not be assessed for this check — the compatibility URL returned an HTTP 406 refusal 35. So whether it exposes rank, organisation or a motherboard QVL is unestablished here, and nothing on this page should be read as a claim about it. Its XPOWER site does claim "QVL certification from leading brands like MSI and GIGABYTE demonstrates Silicon Power’s established market reputation" 34, without linking a list.

DeeperThe detail

Two things a buyer would reasonably expect are missing in a way that matters.

Temperature, on consumer parts. No operating or storage range appears on the DDR4 UDIMM or DDR4 SODIMM pages 31 28. For a laptop module in a hot climate — which is most of India for most of the year — that is the specification a buyer would want most, and it is not there.

Rank and organisation, on consumer parts. The RDIMM pages do give chip density 32, but the consumer UDIMM and SODIMM pages give neither rank nor organisation 27 31 28. Those two facts determine whether a second module will work alongside the first, so their absence lands directly on the practical question.

And with no part-number decoder published 10 9 29, a buyer cannot recover them from the part number either. Two DDR4 RDIMM parts differ only by a B02 versus F02 suffix at the same capacity 32 — almost certainly a meaningful difference in construction, and nowhere explained.

ExpertFor specialists

The documentation that would close these gaps appears to exist and could not be opened. The downloads page lists specification files with versions, dates and file sizes — "DDR5 RDIMM | EN | v1.1 | 2026/03 | 1.88 MB", "XPOWER Cyclone R DDR5 RGB UDIMM | EN | v1.0 | 2026/5 | 2.73 MB" — but exposed metadata only, with no resolvable links in what rendered for this check 29.

So this page states what it can: Silicon Power publishes specification documents whose contents are unestablished here. It does not claim they lack rank or organisation data, because that was never observable. The only claim made is about what the web pages carry.

The e-catalogue page lists four current catalogues — "2026 CreatePro Product Catalogue" at v2.0, "XPOWER Product Brochure" at v1.0, "Consumer Products" at v5.0 and "Consumer Accessories" at v4.0 — and, notably, no industrial catalogue 30. Consistent with the industrial material having moved to the domain that cannot be validated 36.

No JEDEC raw-card reference, no SPD byte detail and no die-organisation table was found on any of the pages read for this check 27 32 29 30.

One honest note on the site’s overall reliability, since several findings on this page turn on pages that would not load. Silicon Power’s support subdomain returned HTTP 406 on its service and compatibility paths, and its industrial domain fails TLS validation entirely 35 36. The warranty page itself renders fine. Where this guide reports an absence, it names the pages read; where a page refused to load, it says so instead.

9.Official pricing

SimpleStart here

Silicon Power publishes no prices on its own site. No product page read for this check carries a price column, and the purchase page is a dealer directory rather than a shop 37 27 31.

That is the normal case in this section, not an omission — almost every company here publishes none, and this site never guesses one.

What it does publish is who is allowed to sell to you, and why that matters: "Authorized dealers with official manufacturer licensing provide consumers with reliable product services and original manufacturer warranty coverage for future repair needs." 37

Read that as a warning, because the warranty page turns it into one. See below.

DeeperThe detail

The purchase directory covers about a hundred countries and regions and routes to a mix of marketplaces, retailers and distributors 37. Its structure is per-country: India, for instance, has its own path 38.

The warranty clause that gives the dealer directory its teeth is on the support site, in the list of exclusions: coverage does not apply to "Products purchased through unauthorized distribution channels or secondhand resale markets." 35

So the two pages work together. Silicon Power names the authorised channels 37, and excludes from warranty anything bought outside them 35. For a market with heavy grey-market and parallel-import trade — India very much included — that pairing is the single most practically important thing on this guide, and it is covered further in the India section.

On the warranty itself, the table’s memory row pairs "SP Memory Module" with the term "Limited Lifetime warranty" 35. Note the lower-case "w": the same company prints "Limited Lifetime Warranty" on its DDR5 UDIMM, DDR5 RDIMM, DDR4 RDIMM and both XPOWER pages, and lower-case on its DDR4 UDIMM and DDR4 SODIMM pages 27 26 33 23 31 28. Harmless in itself, and a fair indicator of how closely the pages are kept in step.

ExpertFor specialists

Now the clause this guide would single out above all others on the page. Silicon Power’s definition of "lifetime" is circular.

"For limited Lifetime Warranty products, the warranty service starting from the original purchase date until the end of product life cycle. (The end of product life cycle is determined by SP)." 35

Reproduced exactly as printed, including the mid-sentence capitalisation and the ungrammatical "the warranty service starting from" — both are the source’s own.

"Product life cycle" is not defined. The page says only that Silicon Power decides when it ends. Checked the whole warranty page, both FAQ pages and all seven product pages 35 10 9 27 31 28 26 32 33 23: no year cap, no end-of-life notice mechanism and no published discontinuation list. So the term is: cover lasts until SP says the life cycle has ended, and the company publishes neither a definition nor a notice mechanism.

Compare the three definitions now on the record in this section. PNY: "the lifetime of the product on the market" — explicit, and it means the market life of the line. Patriot: as long as Patriot still makes the part, with substitution afterwards — explicit, with a remedy. Silicon Power: until the end of the product life cycle, undefined. Of the three, it is the only one a buyer cannot evaluate at all.

Two further clauses worth having. On what you get back: "SP will either replace it with a refurbished、new one or repair it to be functionally equivalent to a new one. Hence, the repaired product that you receive might not be the one you originally sent." 35 — the CJK comma is as printed. On proof: registration is not required; "consumers should provide one of the following supporting documents to verify the original purchase date of the product.", with registration details being one acceptable option among several 35.

On transferability, this page records an ambiguity rather than an answer. No clause limiting the warranty to the original purchaser or declaring it non-transferable was found 35 — but the term is anchored to "the original purchase date", and nothing states whether a second owner inherits it. Unresolved on the published record, and this guide does not resolve it either way.

The only regional variation anywhere in the warranty table is for power banks, "13 months, 24 months (EU)" 35. Memory carries no regional qualifier at all — which, unlike PNY, at least means an Indian buyer and an American buyer are promised the same thing.

10.Whose chips are inside

SimpleStart here

Silicon Power has no HBM position. HBM is made by Samsung, SK hynix and Micron, all of whom own fabs; Silicon Power owns none.

Its AI involvement is real but sits at the other end of the machine. Its March 2026 RDIMM launch is pitched at "intensive workloads such as artificial intelligence, machine learning, and large-scale data processing" 17, and its COMPUTEX 2026 theme was "InSPire with AI", where "SP Industrial’s industrial-grade storage solutions provide the essential foundation for stable AI system performance" 21.

So: server memory and industrial storage for AI systems, not the high-bandwidth memory that sits on the accelerators themselves.

DeeperThe detail

The enterprise RDIMM is the closest thing to a data-centre memory position, and it is worth taking seriously because it is new and specific. Eight parts, 16 to 64GB, 4800 to 6400 MT/s 26, with on-die ECC and a registering clock driver, "significantly enhancing fault tolerance compared to standard ECC UDIMMs" 17, "fully compliant with JEDEC DDR5 standards and has undergone extensive compatibility testing with mainstream Intel and AMD server platforms" 17.

Note what that is and is not. A DDR5 RDIMM is the memory that goes in a server’s DIMM slots — the same DRAM technology a desktop uses, buffered and error-corrected. HBM is a different product entirely: stacked die bonded onto the accelerator package, sold by the three makers to NVIDIA and AMD. Silicon Power competes in the first market and is absent from the second.

The COMPUTEX release names actual design-in partners for the industrial side, with model numbers — "AAEON BOXER-8645AI" for "in-vehicle AI systems" and "ADLINK DLAP-411-Orin" for "smart factory applications" 21. That is a more concrete customer disclosure than most brands in this section offer.

But read it precisely: that release is about industrial storage. It does not mention industrial DRAM modules, wide-temperature ranges, long-term supply or any DRAM chip supplier 21.

ExpertFor specialists

The question worth asking of any module brand in 2026 is what the memory shortage does to it, and Silicon Power publishes nothing that answers it.

Checked the news-index headlines across its six pages, the three 2026 memory-related releases, the business page, the About page and the investor pages 16 17 19 21 15 1 11. The only thing on the reachable site that touches supply is a single line on the business page: "Silicon Power maintains robust inventory management and real-time price updates, ensuring stable supply even during raw material shortages." 15 It is a channel-facing assurance rather than a disclosure — no allocation position, no pricing commentary, no named component source. For a company that must be buying die in the tightest market in years, and that is listed and therefore filing, the silence on its own website is notable — though the filings themselves are the Google Drive documents this check could not open 7.

So this section records an absence and refuses to fill it. Silicon Power launched a new enterprise module in March 2026, in the middle of a price surge, and says nothing publicly about either the surge or its supply. Whether the timing was strategic or opportunistic is not established by anything it publishes.

One thing that can be said, and it points at the whole problem this section keeps circling. The single sourcing phrase Silicon Power publishes — "original high-quality DRAM chips" 17 — is the kind of claim that only matters in a tight market. When die is scarce and remarked or downgraded silicon circulates, asserting original chips is a real commitment. Asserting it without naming the maker leaves a buyer unable to check it, and Silicon Power names no maker anywhere 17 26 27.

11.Who buys it and why

SimpleStart here

Silicon Power sells to three groups, and its catalogue shows which it takes most seriously.

  1. Consumers — plain SP UDIMM and SODIMM, DDR3 through DDR5, for upgrades 24 25.
  2. Gamers — the XPOWER lines, thirteen families, with RGB, a 10-layer PCB claim and ROG certification on the newest 20 34 19.
  3. Servers — two RDIMM families 18. Only the DDR5 RDIMM has a dated announcement, 19 March 2026 17; the DDR4 RDIMM appears in the catalogue with no launch date published.

Alongside those sits SP Industrial, a separate business with its own domain, which this check could not read at all 36.

DeeperThe detail

The industrial arm is acknowledged on the main site without being described there. The About page includes "industrial memory products" in what the company makes 1, the RDIMM boilerplate ends with "DRAM modules, and industrial-grade solutions" 17, and the main navigation links "SP Industrial" off to its own domain 2.

That domain could not be retrieved for this check. Every attempt failed on TLS validation inside this check’s own network, so nothing is established about the server’s configuration 36. So Silicon Power’s entire industrial technical documentation — which is where wide-temperature parts, long-term availability and any bill-of-materials commitment would be described — is unread here. This guide makes no claim about what it contains.

What can be said about the industrial customers is what the COMPUTEX release names: design-ins at AAEON (the BOXER-8645AI, for in-vehicle AI) and ADLINK (the DLAP-411-Orin, for smart factories), for "heavy workloads and variable operating environments" 21. Naming customers with model numbers is unusual and to the company’s credit — and both examples are storage rather than DRAM 21.

For the retail buyer, the memory FAQ is the practical document: eleven questions covering what to do when a machine will not start after a memory upgrade, how to install modules, how to check specifications, how to tell if RAM is faulty, whether mixed capacities and speeds can be used together, XMP, why only half the memory shows, and why a kit does not run at its advertised speed 10.

ExpertFor specialists

On the question that matters most to a buyer adding memory later, Silicon Power answers a narrower question than the one being asked.

Its guidance: "Mixing memory modules with different speeds or capacities can cause stability issues, lower performance, or even prevent your computer from starting" — and then it qualifies the capacity half: "Mixing different memory sizes is possible but may disrupt dual-channel mode, resulting in reduced performance." 10 It also advises "It’s best to use RAM sticks with the same speed (e.g., 3200MHz) to maintain optimal performance" and "Aim for a dual-channel setup—like 8GB x 2—for better performance." 10

All of that is about mixing different specifications. None of it addresses the case a buyer of a second identical module is actually in: two modules of the same part number, bought at different times, which may contain different DRAM. Checked the memory FAQ, the full FAQ index and all seven product pages 10 9 27 31 28 26 32 33 23. No statement found on buying a second module of the same Silicon Power part number.

Some brands in this section do answer that question, and warn against it precisely because the silicon inside a fixed part number can change — see their own guides. Silicon Power neither warns nor reassures.

One asymmetry between its buyer groups is worth naming, though not the one you would guess. The enterprise RDIMM page and the XPOWER gaming pages both carry a subject-to-change clause 26 33 23. The plain SP UDIMM and SODIMM pages carry none 27 31 28 — so the buyer given the least notice is the one buying the cheapest ordinary upgrade part.

The practical advice this guide gives is the same for Silicon Power as for every brand here: buy all the memory you intend to own in a single kit, in one box, at one time — and, given the warranty exclusion for unauthorised channels 35, buy it from a dealer the company itself names 37.

12.Hidden in plain sight

SimpleStart here

The thing hiding in plain sight is that a listed company publishes no numbers on its own website.

Silicon Power has traded on the Taipei Exchange since 2012 3, which means audited financial statements exist. Its financial-information page contains no figures at all — only links to Google Drive files 7, none of which could be opened for this check. Its shareholder list is hosted the same way 8.

So: revenue, the DRAM-versus-storage split, and who owns the company are all unestablished from its own site, and this guide states no figure for any of them.

DeeperThe detail

The second is that more than half the memory range is on page two or three of its own catalogue. The memory index offers three numbered pages and the XPOWER index two 22 20, and four of the six DDR4 XPOWER families — the non-RGB Zenith DDR4, Turbine, Turbine RGB and AirCool — sit past the first screen. Only Pulse DDR4 and Zenith RGB DDR4 make the first page. A buyer who does not notice the pagination sees nine of twenty-three families.

Naming inconsistency compounds it. The brand site promotes the line as "Turbine DDR4 RGB" 34 while the product itself is titled "XPOWER Turbine RGB DDR4 Gaming UDIMM" 23 — the tokens inverted between two of the company’s own pages.

ExpertFor specialists

The fourth is the quality claim frozen in time. "With strict quality control, we have won the Taiwan Excellence Award for nine consecutive years as of 2016." 1 A 2026 About page anchoring its flagship quality proof to a run it last updated ten years ago — though the company has not stopped winning the award: its own news index carries "Silicon Power’s XPOWER Storm DDR5 RGB Wins Taiwan Excellence Award 2026", dated 19 November 2025 16. The gap is editorial rather than substantive.

Its other third-party credentials are design awards rather than quality certifications — the business page names the German iF Design Award, the Red Dot Design Award, the US IDEA Industrial Design Excellence Award and Japan’s GOOD DESIGN AWARD 15, and the news index carries a Red Dot 2026 and an iF Design Award 2026 for the XPOWER Cyclone DDR5 RGB 16. So the company is not short of recognition; what stops at 2016 is the About page’s own nine-consecutive-years sentence, not the award itself 16. Its ISO claims carry no certificate number or registrar 15, and its QVL claim names brands without linking a list 34.

The fifth is the naming drift inside a single product family, which will cost someone a wrong purchase. "XPOWER Zenith DDR5 RGB Gaming UDIMM" against "XPOWER Zenith RGB DDR4 Gaming UDIMM" 20 — the RGB token moves relative to the DDR generation within the same sub-brand. And the brand site’s short name "Turbine DDR4 RGB" inverts the order in its own product URL 34 23.

The sixth, and the one this guide would fix first if it could: SP Industrial’s site could not be retrieved here 36. Every attempt failed inside this check’s own network, before any page was requested. No claim is made about the certificate Silicon Power’s server presents to an ordinary browser — a reader following the "SP Industrial" link from the main navigation may well reach it perfectly well. This is a limit on this guide, not a finding about the company, and it is the reason a whole section reads "unestablished".

Finally, the support subdomain is unreliable in a way that affects buyers and not just researchers. Its warranty page renders 35, but its service and compatibility paths returned HTTP 406 refusals, and the India support link on its own locations page is one of the paths that fails 4 35. See the India section.

13.Weak spots

SimpleStart here

The honest list.

  1. "Lifetime" is defined as whatever the company decides — "until the end of product life cycle", and "(The end of product life cycle is determined by SP)" 35.
  2. Whose DRAM is inside is never stated. The closest is "original high-quality DRAM chips", naming nobody 17.
  3. No part-number decoder, though full part numbers are published on the product pages — SP016GBLFU320F02 against SP016GBLFU320X02, where the suffix evidently encodes something and nothing published says what 31 28.
  4. A listed company publishing no figures on its own site 7.
  5. A "Wide" temperature row whose value is the ordinary commercial range, 0 to 85 °C 32.
  6. Its own About page claims a Hong Kong subsidiary its locations page does not list 1 4.
  7. Its industrial site could not be retrieved for this check, taking a whole product line out of reach — a limit on this guide, not a published shortcoming 36.
DeeperThe detail

Taking the most consequential ones in turn.

The warranty definition. "For limited Lifetime Warranty products, the warranty service starting from the original purchase date until the end of product life cycle. (The end of product life cycle is determined by SP)." 35 Of the three definitions of "lifetime" now on record in this section — PNY’s "the lifetime of the product on the market", Patriot’s production-life-with-substitution, and this — Silicon Power’s is the only one a buyer cannot evaluate, because the term it turns on is defined only as whatever Silicon Power decides, with no notice mechanism or discontinued-product list published 35 9 10.

The unauthorised-channel exclusion. Coverage excludes "Products purchased through unauthorized distribution channels or secondhand resale markets." 35 Combined with a dealer directory that names only two authorised channels for India 38, that is a narrow path to a valid warranty in a market where parallel imports are common.

The subsidiary contradiction. The About page lists subsidiaries in "the Netherlands, Japan, the United States, Hong Kong, and India" 1; the Asia locations page lists Taiwan, Japan, China, India, Vietnam, Philippines and Indonesia, with no Hong Kong entry, and the Europe page adds a German office the About page omits 4 12. The two lists disagree in both directions, and neither gives a registered entity name for any of them.

The temperature label. "Operating Temp. (Wide)" against a value of "0~85°C" 32. A wide-temperature part means roughly −40 °C at the bottom; 0 °C is commercial. The label is misleading as printed and carries no footnote.

ExpertFor specialists

The remaining gaps, each with the pages that were read to establish it:

  1. No IC-substitution policy in either direction. Nothing states whether the DRAM inside a fixed part number may change. Checked seven product pages and both FAQ indexes 27 31 28 26 32 33 23 10 9. The change clauses that exist are on the enterprise and XPOWER pages and concern specifications, not components; the plain SP pages carry none 26 33 27.
  2. No rank or organisation on consumer parts, so compatibility cannot be reasoned about 27 31 28.
  3. No operating temperature on consumer parts at all 31 28 — which for a hot-climate market is the specification most worth having.
  4. Unauditable ISO claims. ISO 9001 and ISO 14001 asserted with no certificate number, registrar, scope or validity date 15.
  5. No binning or IC-screening disclosure. The enthusiast pages claim testing — "100% factory-tested" 33 and "100% testing across major motherboard brands" 34 — but say nothing about how die are selected, sorted or graded.
  6. No named executive anywhere on the reachable site; the government registry supplies the responsible person that the company does not 6 1 11.
  7. Datasheets that appear to exist and could not be opened — the downloads page exposes metadata without resolvable links 29.
  8. A support subdomain returning HTTP 406 on its service and compatibility paths, including the path its own India entry links to 35 4.

Two points of fairness, because this list is long and the record is not one-sided. Silicon Power publishes a factory street address 4, corroborated by an RMA address on the same block 35 — which most module brands in this section do not. It states ISO certifications at all 15, which several do not. It names industrial design-in customers with model numbers 21. And it is listed and audited 3, so the numbers exist even though its website does not carry them.

And one limit on this whole guide, stated once more because it is load-bearing: SP Industrial could not be read at all 36. Where this page reports that Silicon Power publishes nothing about bill-of-materials control, long-term availability or wide-temperature DRAM, that is a statement about the reachable site. Nothing here should be read as a finding about what SP Industrial does or does not promise its customers.

14.India angle

SimpleStart here

Silicon Power does better on India than most brands in this section, and then stops short in a way that matters.

What it publishes: an India office, on its own About page, in a list of five locations — "Taiwan 丨 Taipei", "Japan 丨 Tokyo", "India 丨 Bangalore", "Netherlands 丨 Amsterdam", "USA 丨 California" 14. India is also named as a subsidiary 1, and its Asia locations page gives India an entry headed "India Support RMA Centre" 4.

What it does not publish: any Indian address, phone number, email, entity name or hours. The Bangalore entry is a city name. The RMA centre entry is a heading and a link 4.

The separator in that location list is the CJK character 丨, not a pipe. It is the company’s own 14.

DeeperThe detail

It names an Indian dealer, which is genuinely notable — Patriot was the only brand in this section to do so before now. Its India purchase page lists exactly two authorised channels 38:

  1. Amazon IN
  2. Prime ABGB Pvt. Ltd.

No address or phone is given for either 38.

Now put that beside the warranty exclusion, because together they are the most practically important thing on this page for an Indian reader. Silicon Power excludes from warranty "Products purchased through unauthorized distribution channels or secondhand resale markets" 35. It names two authorised channels for India 38. So a Silicon Power module bought from any other Indian seller — and there are many — is, on the company’s own published terms, outside warranty.

One thing Silicon Power does not do, and it is to its credit: it applies no India-specific warranty term. The only regional carve-out anywhere in its warranty table is for power banks, "13 months, 24 months (EU)" 35. Memory is "Limited Lifetime warranty" everywhere, with no regional qualifier. An Indian buyer and an American buyer are promised the same thing — which is not true of PNY, whose India site links a document granting ten years against product pages advertising a lifetime.

ExpertFor specialists

The India entry on the locations page deserves quoting in full, because its brevity is the finding. Under the India heading, the entire content is the words "India Support RMA Centre" and a link to a customer-service page 4.

Four Asian territories get only a heading and a link — India, Vietnam, the Philippines and Indonesia, all four identical in form, India’s sub-heading merely being the more specific "India Support RMA Centre" rather than "Service Centre" 4. The territories that get more are Taiwan (head office, factory, phone, fax and a service-centre number), Japan (two street addresses), China (two addresses with phones), the Netherlands (address, phone and email), Germany (an address) and the United States (address and email) 4 12 13. So India is not singled out; it is one of four territories Silicon Power documents with a hyperlink.

And the hyperlink does not work from here. The service path it points to returned an HTTP 406 refusal on every attempt, and the warranty page that does render is a policy document: it gives a US support email and phone number and a Taiwan RMA address, but no Indian contact of any kind 35. And the hyperlink is not India-specific: it points to the same generic consult form that Vietnam, the Philippines and Indonesia link to 4. That path returned an HTTP 406 refusal on every attempt here, so the one route published to an India RMA centre could not be followed for this check.

A fair caveat on that: an HTTP 406 is a server refusing a particular client, and a person in a browser may well reach the page perfectly well. This is reported as a retrieval failure, not as a broken link for everyone. What can be said is that the India RMA centre’s contact details are not published anywhere this check could read.

Two further findings, both recorded with their uncertainty intact.

A New Delhi service centre was announced in 2011 and the page is gone. A press release titled "Silicon Power opens new Service Center in New Delhi showing long-term commitment to the Indian market" is still indexed on the company’s own domain; the URL returns 404, the legacy news tree having been retired. The content was never read for this check, so this page records only that the title exists and the page does not — and that no current India service address is published 39 4.

No Indian legal entity could be identified. The About page asserts an India subsidiary 1 but names none, and the annual reports that would list subsidiaries are the Google Drive files that could not be opened 7. A search surfaced an unrelated Indian company with a similar name in power electronics, registered in 1992 — almost certainly not this company, and it is deliberately not cited here. So the entity is asserted by Silicon Power and unidentified by this guide.

Finally, no India-specific product pages, no rupee prices, and no /in/ path or Indian subdomain: the site offers language variants for Chinese, Japanese, Italian, Polish, French, Burmese and German, and the only India-scoped page is the dealer directory 2 38. No statement about manufacturing in India or any Indian government scheme was found on the About page, the Chinese About page, the business page or the news index 1 14 15 16.

15.What’s next

SimpleStart here

Two things are demonstrably in motion, both dated 2026.

Enterprise memory. The DDR5 RDIMM announced on 19 March 2026 is pitched at AI and machine-learning workloads, and the release frames it as the company "expanding its enterprise memory portfolio" rather than entering the market — its "latest DDR5 RDIMM module" 17. A DDR4 RDIMM is also catalogued, with no published launch date 18.

Certified gaming memory. The XPOWER Cyclone R DDR5, announced 26 May 2026, is ROG certified 19 — building on the XPOWER DDR5 CUDIMM introduced in May 2025 16, the clocked module type that arrived with Intel’s newest platforms 20.

A long-standing consumer and gaming memory brand entering registered server memory, and refreshing its gaming line, within about two months.

Selected announcements from 2025 and 2026, from the company’s own news index — which runs to six pages, so this is not its full record 16:

What is not published is any roadmap. No statement was found about DDR5 speeds beyond its current range, about CAMM2, about DDR6, or about capacity or supply plans — checked the news-index headlines across its six pages, the three 2026 memory-related releases, the business page and the investor pages 16 17 19 21 15 11. Its XPOWER Cyclone DDR5 RGB CUDIMM product page already lists tested speeds up to 9200 MT/s 40, which is above JEDEC’s standard grades and therefore overclocking territory.

ExpertFor specialists

Three things a reader should watch, chosen because each would resolve something this guide had to leave open.

  1. Whether SP Industrial’s site becomes retrievable from a build environment like this one. Until it does, its entire published technical record — wide-temperature parts, long-term availability, any bill-of-materials commitment — is unreadable to a validating client, and a whole section of this guide stays at "unestablished" 36.
  2. Whether "product life cycle" ever gets defined. It is the term the warranty turns on, and undefining it is the single biggest weakness in the company’s consumer-facing documents 35.
  3. Whether the RDIMM line grows past two families. A two-item server catalogue 18 is an entry, not a business. If it broadens, the March 2026 launch was strategic; if it does not, it was a moment in a high-priced market.

And one thing this page will not predict. Silicon Power is listed, audited and filing 3, and its numbers are therefore knowable — just not from its own website, which publishes none 7. Anyone wanting to judge where this company is going should read the filings rather than the guide, and this guide says so rather than guessing at trajectory from press releases.

The pattern the evidence does support, stated as narrowly as the sources allow: a storage brand of twenty-three years, listed for fourteen, that publishes a factory address and ISO certifications, names its industrial design-in customers, launched both a new enterprise module and certified gaming memory during 2026, and tells its consumers nothing at all about the silicon inside its modules — naming no DRAM supplier on any page read here, and publishing neither rank nor organisation on its consumer parts.
