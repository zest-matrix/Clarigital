XPG ARMAX DDR5: The Complete Guide

ADATA’s XPG brand launched ARMAX DDR5 in December 2025 and says plainly what it is for — the “performance sweet spot,” and small builds. Its own LANCER BLADE line does both, in a smaller module. Simple to deep dive, every number links to its source.

1.At a glance

SimpleStart here

ARMAX DDR5 is XPG’s gaming memory series launched on 3 December 2025, in two versions: “the ARMAX RGB DDR5 with dazzling lighting effects” and “the understated, low-key ARMAX DDR5” 1. XPG is a brand by ADATA 1. The spec tab lists 16GB and 32GB modules at tested speeds of 5600, 6000 and 6400 MT/s 2.

Deep diveThe technical detail

The launch release headline is “XPG Launches New ARMAX DDR5 Gaming Memory Series,” under the subtitle “Stealth Fighter Design Helps Gamers Conquer Every Battle” 1. Its first section is headed “Targeting the DDR5 Performance Sweet Spot with SFF Compatibility,” and states that “Performance-wise, the series locks into the ‘performance sweet spot’ of next-generation platforms with speeds ranging from 6,000 to 6,400 MT/s” 1. Both the plain and RGB spec tabs give the same electrical figures: capacity 16GB and 32GB, test speeds 5600, 6000 and 6400 MT/s, test CAS latencies 30, 32 and 46, test operating voltage 1.1V–1.4V, and an SPD default of 4800 MT/s at 40-39-39-77 and 1.1V 23. XPG’s desktop memory listing carries ARMAX and ARMAX RGB as two of nine DDR5 series it sells 4.

2.Launch and lineup

SimpleStart here

ARMAX arrived in December 2025 and did not replace anything — XPG’s own desktop memory listing still carries LANCER, LANCER BLADE, LANCER RGB and NOVAKEY RGB alongside it, nine DDR5 series in all 4.

Deep diveThe technical detail

The launch release opens: “XPG, a fast-growing provider of systems, components, and peripherals for Gamers, Esports Pros, and Tech Enthusiasts, today announces the launch of the new ARMAX DDR5 gaming memory series” 1. It names no executive and carries no attributed quotation — unusual for a memory launch release, and different from the pattern on other brands covered on this site, where a DRAM business manager is normally quoted. It states no price and no capacity figures, saying only that the series “is available now through global channels and comes with a limited lifetime warranty,” and closes on the aim of “the premier DDR5 upgrade choice for next-gen gamers to conquer all gaming campaigns” 1. XPG’s desktop memory listing as checked in September 2026 shows nine DDR5 series live at once. The first eight run in this order: NOVAKEY RGB, ARMAX RGB, ARMAX, LANCER RGB GOLDEN HORSE EDITION, LANCER BLADE, LANCER BLADE RGB, LANCER RGB and LANCER; a HUNTER DDR5 SO-DIMM follows further down, after three DDR4 tiles 4.

3.What’s inside the module

SimpleStart here

The heatsink is the selling point. XPG calls it a “fighter-jet-inspired heatsink” with “muscular lines” and a “battle-ready look” 2, and the launch release heads its design section “Stealth Fighter Jet Aesthetics: RGB Lighting for Personalized Style” 1.

Deep diveThe technical detail

XPG gives a functional reason for the height, not only a stylistic one: “the ARMAX DDR5 features a heatsink height of just 39.5mm. This design avoids interference with large CPU coolers, cables, or other equipment, allowing for easy installation in various small chassis” 1. On lighting, the release describes “a V-shaped top light bar that focuses the visual attention on the center of the heatsink, creating a dynamic ‘diving’ effect,” and says the RGB version “supports XPG PRIME and major motherboard lighting control software” 1. The spec tabs list both versions in Black only — no white or silver option is shown for either 23, where LANCER RGB DDR5 is offered in black or white 5. The two ARMAX versions differ by fractions of a millimetre and a fraction of a gram: 133.35 x 39.49 x 8.5mm at 43.16g / 46.74g for the plain ARMAX, versus 133.35 x 39.5 x 8.5mm at 43.28g / 46.86g for ARMAX RGB 23 — the light bar adds roughly a tenth of a gram, not a taller module. Whether 39.5mm is short enough for a given case is a clearance question, not a spec question; this site’s will it fit page covers how to check.

4.Full spec table

SimpleStart here

XPG’s own sources disagree slightly on the speed range: the launch release says “speeds ranging from 6,000 to 6,400 MT/s” 1, while both spec tabs list 5600, 6000 and 6400 MT/s as tested speeds 23 and the category listing gives “5600~6400MT/s｜CL30~46” 4. Two of the three agree, so this page uses 5600–6400.

5.Where it’s made

SimpleStart here

Neither the launch release nor either spec tab names who fabricates the DRAM dies inside ARMAX DDR5 123. XPG goes as far as “Built with strictly selected high-quality ICs” 1 and, on the product page, “strict component selection and screening procedures” 2 — and no further.

Deep diveThe technical detail

ADATA is a module maker, not a fabricator — it buys DRAM dies and assembles them into modules. None of the XPG sources checked for this page names a die supplier for ARMAX 1234. XPG’s two statements on the subject stop at the sourcing process rather than the source: modules are “Built with strictly selected high-quality ICs” 1 and have “undergone strict component selection and screening procedures” 2. That silence is the norm rather than the exception — see whose dies are inside for how far each module brand goes. What XPG does claim about the silicon is feature-level: modules carry a “built-in Power Management IC (PMIC) that enhance power supply stability” and “On-die error correcting code (ECC)” for “increased stability and reliability” 2, and the launch release pairs the same two features 1. Both are required by the DDR5 standards rather than added by XPG: DDR5 DIMMs carry an on-module PMIC in place of the motherboard-side regulation DDR4 used, and DDR5 DRAM devices must implement on-die ECC. On-die ECC is also narrower than the name suggests — it corrects errors inside a chip before the data leaves it, and is not the same thing as ECC memory, which adds module-level correction and reports errors to the host. This site’s memory glossary sets out the difference and cites JEDEC’s own DDR5 material for it.

6.What it’s compatible with

SimpleStart here

ARMAX supports both overclocking profiles. XPG states the series “supports Intel® XMP 3.0 and AMD EXPO” 1, and the category listing tags both ARMAX and ARMAX RGB “AMD EXPO/XMP3.0 Overclocking” 4.

Deep diveThe technical detail

Dual XMP-and-EXPO support is stated in the launch release for the series as a whole, without splitting the plain and RGB versions 1, and both appear on the category listing 4. The product page states support for both and separately describes XMP as letting you overclock “without the need to hassle with BIOS settings” — that ease-of-use phrasing is attached to XMP, not to EXPO 2. Dual certification is not universal at this end of the market: some competing flagship lines carry Intel XMP only, and this site’s XMP and EXPO explained page covers what each profile does and why a module can carry one without the other. XPG publishes no motherboard qualified-vendor list on either ARMAX page; the only route offered is a “Find Compatible DRAM/SSD” search tool in XPG’s site-wide navigation, not an ARMAX-specific list 2. The RGB version additionally “supports XPG PRIME and major motherboard lighting control software,” which is a lighting-software compatibility claim rather than a memory-platform one 1.

7.Official pricing

SimpleStart here

XPG publishes no price for ARMAX DDR5. The launch release says only that the series “is available now through global channels and comes with a limited lifetime warranty” 1, and neither spec tab carries a price 23.

Deep diveThe technical detail

No price appears in any XPG source checked for this page — not the launch release, not either product page or spec tab, not the desktop memory listing 1234. This matches every other memory brand this site has checked: module makers state specifications and leave pricing to resellers. ARMAX also launched in December 2025, into the DDR5 price movement covered on this site’s memory price surge page, so any retailer figure quoted here would be stale before it was read. This page states no price rather than an estimate.

8.Rated vs. real-world speed

SimpleStart here

XPG publishes rated speeds and latencies but no benchmark results for ARMAX DDR5 12. Both versions carry a “Limited lifetime warranty” per XPG’s own spec tabs 23.

Deep diveThe technical detail

This page reports XPG’s own published specification claims and does not re-run or cite third-party benchmark results. The only performance figures XPG gives are the rated speed grades and the three tested CAS latencies, none of which is tied to a speed 23. The operating envelope is stated as 0°C to 85°C on both versions 23. On warranty, both spec tabs state “Limited lifetime warranty” and the launch release repeats it 123 — but that is the whole of it. None of those three pages states a term in years, a regional cap, an exclusion list or a claims procedure, and neither product page links to warranty terms from its own content — the only route is XPG’s site-wide “Product Support” and “RMA” footer links 23. See this site’s memory warranty compared page for how that stands against other brands.

9.Where this family sits in the lineup

SimpleStart here

Sits beside, not above: XPG’s desktop memory listing carries ARMAX and ARMAX RGB alongside the LANCER family and NOVAKEY RGB — nine DDR5 series in all, with no tier labels and no stated ranking between them 4.

Deep diveThe technical detail

The closest sibling is not LANCER RGB but LANCER BLADE. XPG’s listing gives both the same speed string — ARMAX “5600~6400MT/s｜CL30~46” against LANCER BLADE “5600~6400MT/s｜CL28~48” — and tags LANCER BLADE separately “Low Profile Heatsink,” a feature line ARMAX’s own tile does not carry 4. On the spec tabs, LANCER BLADE lists capacities of 8GB, 16GB, 24GB and 32GB, test speeds of 4800, 5600, 6000 and 6400 MT/s, test CAS latencies of 28, 30, 32, 36, 46 and 48, test voltage 1.1–1.4V, dimensions of 133.35 x 33.8 x 7.8mm and a weight of 32.9g / 36.2g 6 — against ARMAX’s 16GB and 32GB, 5600–6400 MT/s, 39.49mm height and 43.16g / 46.74g 2. LANCER RGB DDR5 sits clearly above both, at 16GB/32GB/48GB, 6000–8000 MT/s, CAS 30/32/34/38, 1.35–1.40V, 133.35 x 43.56 x 8mm and 72.4g, in black or white 5. So the family reads as LANCER RGB on top, and ARMAX and LANCER BLADE overlapping beneath it at the same speeds.

10.Hidden in plain sight

SimpleStart here

XPG sells ARMAX on a 39.5mm heatsink for small builds 1 — but the line XPG actually labels “Low Profile Heatsink” is LANCER BLADE 4, at 33.8mm 6, nearly 6mm shorter than ARMAX 2, at the same 5600–6400 MT/s and in more capacities 46. Both ARMAX pages list it under “Popular Choices” without ever saying which to buy 23.

Deep diveThe technical detail

XPG is not vague about what ARMAX is for. The launch release heads its first section “Targeting the DDR5 Performance Sweet Spot with SFF Compatibility,” says the series “locks into the ‘performance sweet spot’ of next-generation platforms,” and gives the compactness rationale outright: the 39.5mm height “avoids interference with large CPU coolers, cables, or other equipment, allowing for easy installation in various small chassis” 1. The RGB product page goes further, under the heading “First Choice of New Gamer”: “ARMAX RGB delivers speeds up to 6,400 MT/s, making it the perfect starting point for new gamers” 3. That is an entry-level positioning statement in XPG’s own words.

What XPG never does is explain the difference between ARMAX and the sibling it overlaps. LANCER BLADE covers the same 5600–6400 MT/s span on the same listing page, carries a wider tested latency range (CL28~48 against CL30~46), and is the line XPG itself tags “Low Profile Heatsink” 4. Its spec tab shows it is 33.8mm tall and 32.9g / 36.2g, against ARMAX’s 39.49mm and 43.16g / 46.74g, and it is sold in four capacities from 8GB to 32GB where ARMAX offers two 62. On the one axis ARMAX’s launch release leads with — fitting into a small chassis — LANCER BLADE is the smaller module, by nearly 6mm and about 10g. XPG is not hiding this: both ARMAX product pages put LANCER BLADE DDR5 in front of the reader themselves, listed under “Popular Choices” 23, and the category listing shows the two a few tiles apart 4. What is missing is any statement of how they differ or which a buyer should choose — not on either ARMAX page, not on the listing, and not in the launch release, which never mentions LANCER at all 1234. This page reports that gap rather than guessing at why XPG sells both.

One smaller inconsistency in the same material, worth noting because it cuts the other way: XPG’s listing gives LANCER BLADE as “5600~6400MT/s” while LANCER BLADE’s own spec tab adds a 4800 MT/s grade 46 — a listing-versus-spec-tab mismatch alongside the release-versus-spec-tab one this page records for ARMAX above. XPG’s own pages are not always consistent with each other, which is part of why every figure here is tied to the specific page it came from.
