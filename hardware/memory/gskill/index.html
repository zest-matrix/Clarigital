3.The story

SimpleStart here

G.Skill’s own history page cannot be read from here. What follows is what a registry, a standards body, and two federal court dockets record.

  • 1989 The Taiwan registry's establishment date for 芝奇國際實業股份有限公司 corresponds to 24 April 1989 3.
  • 2006 GSKILL USA, INC. files the U.S. "G.SKILL" word mark for computer memory hardware 8.
  • 2007 That mark registers, No. 3,282,000 8.
  • 2014 The Taiwan parent files two design marks — a shield-and-phoenix mark and a shield-and-unicorn mark — for overclocking-competition services 7 9.
  • 2022 Two federal lawsuits are filed within months of each other, one in California and one in New York, both over DDR4/DDR5 speed labeling; the New York case is stayed in favor of the California one 11 12. The shield-and-unicorn mark is separately cancelled for a filing lapse 9.
  • 2026 The California case settles for $2.4 million, with a final approval hearing set for 5 June 2026 11 13.
DeeperThe detail

The two 2022 lawsuits are the best-documented episode in G.Skill’s history from where this page can check. Tristan Hurd, et al. v. G. Skill International Enterprise Co., LTD., G.SKILL USA, INC., NEUTECK, INC., and RACERSPEED, INC. was filed 31 January 2022 in the U.S. District Court for the Central District of California as No. 2:22-cv-00685 10. The settlement agreement's own caption names all four defendants — the Taiwan parent, its U.S. entity, and two further companies, NeuTeck, Inc. and Racerspeed, Inc., neither of which is otherwise described anywhere read for this guide 11 14. A materially similar case, Nelson v. Gskill USA, Inc., No. 6:22-cv-06175-FPG, was filed separately in the Western District of New York and stayed on 8 May 2023 in the California case’s favor under the "first-filed rule," the two cases alleging the same underlying pattern: that G.Skill's advertised memory speeds required overclocking or BIOS adjustments to reach 12.

The claim, in the settlement documents’ own words, was that G.Skill "deceptively advertised and labeled the speed" of its memory, leading buyers "to believe that the advertised speeds were 'out of the box' speeds requiring no adjustments to their PCs" 13. G.Skill’s response, in the settlement agreement it signed, is an express denial: "Defendants expressly deny any liability or wrongdoing of any kind or that Plaintiffs or any putative Class Member has been damaged in any amount or at all…" 11

ExpertFor specialists

The settlement, reached without an admission of liability, covers "all individuals in the United States who purchased one or more Products from January 31, 2018 to the date the Court grants Preliminary Approval" 11, with the long-form notice dating the class period specifically to 31 January 2018 through 7 January 2026 and defining "Products" as "any G.Skill DDR-4 (non-SODIMM/laptop) memory product with a rated speed over 2133 megahertz (MHz) or any G.Skill DDR-5 (non-SODIMM/laptop) memory product with a rated speed over 4800 megahertz" — laptop SODIMM memory is expressly excluded from both 14 13. The fund is "two million four hundred thousand dollars ($2,400,000.00)", paid into an escrow account, with "Settlement Distributions" paid "on a per-product basis, with a maximum of five (5) qualifying purchases paid per household, without proof of purchase" — a class member claiming more than five must supply proof 11 14. The final approval hearing is set for 5 June 2026 at 2:00 PM Pacific Time, and the settlement is administered through a dedicated site, GSkillDRAMSettlement.com, run by a third-party settlement administrator 13 14.

The going-forward change the agreement describes is a labeling one, not a technical one: G.Skill agreed that where packaging states a rated speed, it will be shown as, for example, "DDR4-3200" followed by an asterisk, with a footer reading "Requires overclocking/BIOS adjustments. Maximum speed and performance depend on system components, including motherboard and CPU." 11 Read carefully, this is the same underlying fact this codex’s own XMP/EXPO explainer already documents industry-wide — that a module’s printed top speed is a profile, not a guaranteed out-of-the-box clock — now written into a specific settlement’s required label text for one brand, following a lawsuit alleging its labeling did not make that clear enough.
