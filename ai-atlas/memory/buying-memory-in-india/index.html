Buying memory in India: what the brands actually promise you

India now assembles memory modules &mdash; Micron&rsquo;s Sanand plant shipped its first in February 2026. But of the brands on an Indian shelf, one manufactures here, one has a registered Indian company with nothing to do with memory, one publishes only a liaison office — a form that, as India’s foreign-exchange rules define it, does not trade — two name a handful of Indian sellers, and the rest publish nothing at all. This page sets out who is actually present, what a warranty is worth here, what Indian law does and does not require of a memory module, and which questions cannot be answered before you buy. Three reading levels, every claim sourced to a company or a government document.

1.Is any of it made in India?

SimpleStart here

As of 2026, yes — partly. Micron’s plant at Sanand, Gujarat was inaugurated on 28 February 2026 and is the first semiconductor assembly and test facility of its kind in the country 1 2.

But read what it does carefully, because the distinction decides what “made in India” means on a memory box. It is not a wafer fab. Micron’s own description is that the plant "converts advanced DRAM and NAND wafers from Micron’s global manufacturing network into finished memory and storage products" 1. The Prime Minister’s Office uses the same framing: the facility will "convert advanced Dynamic Random Access Memory (DRAM) and NAND semiconductor wafers" 3. The silicon is made elsewhere. India does the packaging and the testing.

That is a real industrial step and it is worth being precise about rather than inflating. Nothing published by the Indian government claims DRAM wafer fabrication in India.

DeeperThe detail

The scale and the money are both public. Micron announced the project on 22 June 2023: "Micron’s investment will be up to $825 million over the two phases of the project and will create up to 5,000 new direct Micron jobs and 15,000 community jobs over the next several years", and the combined investment by Micron and the two government entities across both phases is "up to $2.75 billion" 4.

The support is a stated split: "Under the government’s ‘Modified Assembly, Testing, Marking and Packaging (ATMP) Scheme,’ Micron will receive 50% fiscal support for the total project cost from the Indian central government and incentives representing 20% of the total project cost from the state of Gujarat" 4. The Prime Minister’s Office puts the outlay at "more than ₹22,500 crore" and records that this was "the first proposal approved under the India Semiconductor Mission (ISM)" 3.

What the plant is now doing, in Micron’s words at the opening: it expects to "assemble and test tens of millions of chips at Sanand in 2026, scaling to hundreds of millions in 2027", from a phase-one cleanroom of "more than 500,000 square feet" 1. The PMO describes the inauguration as marking "the commencement of commercial production and shipment of the first made-in-India semiconductor memory modules from the Sanand ATMP facility" 3.

For an Indian buyer the practical question is whether any of this reaches a retail memory module, and the honest answer is not directly, not yet. Micron’s announced output is packages, modules and drives for its customers; Micron announced its exit from the Crucial consumer business on 3 December 2025, with shipments running to February 2026 — and that was its retail channel 5. Nothing published names a consumer product line served from Sanand.

ExpertFor specialists

The policy frame matters for what comes next, so it is worth setting out. India’s scheme text reads: "The Scheme for setting up of Compound Semiconductors / Silicon Photonics / Sensors (including MEMS) Fabs / Discrete Semiconductors Fab and Semiconductor ATMP / OSAT facilities in India shall extend fiscal support of 50% of capital expenditure…" 6 — support pitched at packaging and specialist fabs, not at leading-edge DRAM fabrication.

The scale of the wider programme, from the February 2026 announcement of India Semiconductor Mission 2.0: as of December 2025, "10 projects with a total investment of ₹1.60 lakh crore" had been approved across six states, including "advanced and memory packaging facilities", with "₹1,000 crore" allocated for FY 2026–27. The stated goals are the capability to "design and manufacture chips required for nearly 70–75 per cent of domestic applications" by 2029 and "3-nanometre and 2-nanometre technology nodes" by 2035 7.

Note what is absent from that programme, stated as an absence rather than a prediction. Nothing in the ISM material read here describes a DRAM or NAND wafer fabrication initiative, as distinct from packaging. Memory fabs are among the most capital-intensive plants in existence and the three companies that run them have not announced Indian ones. This page makes no forecast either way; it records that the published Indian programme is an assembly-and-packaging programme, and that the PMO’s phrase "the first made-in-India semiconductor memory modules" 3 describes modules built from wafers made abroad.

The Prime Minister’s words at the opening, for the record: "India was once primarily known for software and IT services, it is now rapidly strengthening its identity in the hardware sector." And: "A semiconductor ecosystem is far more than just a factory; it is a multi-layered system comprising machine manufacturers, design engineers, research institutions, logistics networks, and skilled technicians." 2

2.Who is actually here, and in what legal form

SimpleStart here

Of the companies whose memory you can buy in India, here is what each publishes about being here. The legal form matters more than the address, for reasons the next level explains.

CompanyWhat it publishes

SamsungAn Indian company, registered in New Delhi — but nothing about memory here 8

DeeperThe detail

Micron is the only one with a manufacturing company here, and the name comes from the government rather than from Micron: the Prime Minister’s Office records the inauguration of the facility "of Micron Semiconductor Technology India Private Limited in Sanand" 3. A private limited company incorporated in India is the strongest form on this list — it can trade, invoice, employ and be sued here.

Kingston’s Mumbai presence is the most interesting entry, and the most misunderstood. Its contacts page lists the "Kingston Technology India Liaison Office, India 407, Sunteck Crest Near International Airport, Plot No. 3, Next to Leela Business Park, Marol, Andheri (E) Mumbai, Maharashtra 400059" 9. Compare how the same page names its other arms — "Kingston Technology Far East Corp." and "Kingston Technology Co., Ltd" are corporations; the India entry is not 9.

“Liaison office” is a defined legal form under India’s foreign-exchange rules, and the definition is the point. Regulation 2(e) of FEMA 22(R), 2016: a liaison office "means a place of business to act as a channel of communication between the principal place of business or Head Office or by whatever name called and entities in India but which does not undertake any commercial /trading/ industrial activity, directly or indirectly, and maintains itself out of inward remittances received from abroad through normal banking channel." 17 The spacing around "commercial /trading/ industrial" is as published.

A liaison office in that sense is confined to a closed list of activities — representing the parent, promoting trade, acting as a channel of communication — set out in Regulation 4(b) and Schedule II of the same rules 17. Commercial, trading and industrial activity sits outside it. So a properly constituted liaison office is not the entity selling you a module.

One caution on how far that goes, because this is a legal characterisation on a hardware page. The phrase "Liaison Office" is Kingston’s own description on its website, not a registration this site has verified with the Reserve Bank; and Kingston listing no other Indian arm is not the same as there being none. What can fairly be said is narrower: on the face of what Kingston publishes, its India presence is representation rather than trade, and the seller of Kingston memory in India is a distributor.

ExpertFor specialists

Samsung is the case that looks strongest and is least relevant. It publishes an Indian registered office — "6th Floor, DLF Centre, Sansad Marg, New Delhi-110001" — with a corporate identity number, CIN U31900DL1995PTC071387 8. That is a substantial Indian company. But checked its India about page and its India newsroom: no statement was found about memory or semiconductor activity in India 8. Samsung’s large Indian presence is in other businesses.

Two absences recorded properly rather than assumed. SK hynix: its company-history page names operations in the United States, Europe, Singapore, China, Scotland and Korea; the only India mention is a line dated October 2000 recording that it "Entered India’s CDMA WLL equipment market" — a telecom-era market entry, not an office 16. Corsair: its contact page publishes a California headquarters and a Netherlands office and no Indian address 14; its Exhibit 21.1 to the FY2025 Form 10-K, filed 24 February 2026, names 23 subsidiaries and none is organised in India 18. In both cases this is what the company publishes, not a finding about what exists.

PNY is the oddest. It runs an India storefront at an /en-in/ path and carries a "PNY INDIA" label in its regional navigation — but the storefront’s footer carries only a global corporate attribution, and no Indian entity name, office address or registration detail was found on either page 12 13. A country storefront without a country company behind it.

The pattern across the whole table is worth naming. Presence in the Indian memory market is overwhelmingly commercial rather than corporate. One company manufactures here. One has an office forbidden from trading. The rest reach Indian buyers entirely through distributors and retailers they mostly do not name — which makes the next two sections, about who you are allowed to buy from and what your warranty is worth, the practical centre of this page.

3.Who you are allowed to buy from

SimpleStart here

This sounds like a trivial question and is not, because five of the six limit their warranty to modules bought through authorised channels — and most of them will not tell you who those are in India.

Patriot is the exception, and it is the only one. Its India page carries a "Distributors" heading with two companies under it — ANANDIT (ananditinfotech.in) and Shree Computers Sales Pvt Ltd — and beneath them a dozen Indian retailers by name 10.

Silicon Power names two channels for India: Amazon IN and Prime ABGB Pvt. Ltd., given as nothing more than a name and a link 19.

Kingston, Corsair, Lexar and PNY leave an Indian buyer to work it out. That is the gap this page exists to flag, because the warranty depends on getting it right.

DeeperThe detail

Why the list is the warranty. Patriot’s cover applies only to "all Patriot/Viper branded products that were purchased directly from Patriot or through a Patriot authorized reseller", and its exclusions reach "products that are purchased from any unauthorized resellers" 20. For a brand that sells entirely through distribution and never direct, the published list of who is authorised is the only way a buyer can establish they are inside the warranty at all. Kingston’s equivalent clause carries a qualifier worth keeping: "To the extent permitted by applicable laws, products not imported or sold through authorized Kingston dealers or distributors are not covered by this warranty." 21

A data-quality warning about Patriot’s page, because a reader will hit it. The same India page’s retailer block also carries non-Indian entries — a Pakistani retailer, a Philippine one, one listed only by a Facebook page with no country shown, and five Vietnamese ones sit alongside the Indian names 10. The page functions as a regional hub rather than a strictly Indian listing. And the domain does not sort it: only five of the twelve Indian retailers use .in addresses, so the non-Indian entries are identifiable by their own country-code domains rather than by the Indian ones standing out.

Silicon Power’s two entries sit under a single "Online Shop" heading with no descriptor for either 19. Its warranty then makes the list matter: coverage does not extend to "Products purchased through unauthorized distribution channels or secondhand resale markets" 22. Two named channels, and an exclusion for everything outside them.

ExpertFor specialists

The structural problem is sharper in India than in most markets, and it is worth stating plainly rather than implying.

Parallel-imported stock — genuine product brought into a country outside the brand’s own distribution — is a recognised feature of computer retail generally. This site has not measured how common it is in India and cites no source that does. What can be said from the documents is narrower, and is the part that matters: a module bought outside the authorised channel is not counterfeit, and is still excluded from cover by the plain wording of five of the six clauses read here. The distance between “a genuine Kingston module” and “a Kingston module with a valid Kingston warranty” is the seller, not the part.

Which makes the silence of most brands consequential rather than merely untidy. A buyer who wants to stay inside the warranty needs to know the authorised channel; four of the six publish no Indian list; and their warranty documents nonetheless exclude purchases outside it. This page does not characterise that as deliberate — publishing and maintaining a country channel list is real work, and no source read here says why any brand does or does not do it. It records that the obligation is asymmetric: the exclusion is published, the list that would let you satisfy it mostly is not.

One piece of practical advice follows, and it is the only one this page will give on the subject. Ask the seller for a GST invoice in your name, naming the brand and the part number, and keep it. Most of the warranties read for this site require proof of purchase, and several require the purchase to be from an authorised reseller — Kingston "You will be required to provide proof of purchase" 21 and Silicon Power any one of a warranty certificate, proof of purchase or registration details 22. Patriot’s published policy sets no document requirement at all 20 — which does not mean a reseller will not ask for one. An invoice is the single document that does the most work if the module fails, and it is the cheapest thing to get right at the time of buying.

4.What a warranty is worth here

SimpleStart here

The headline term is usually the same everywhere — a lifetime warranty. What it is worth in India differs from what it is worth in the United States, in two ways.

First, the term itself can be shorter here. PNY’s product pages say Limited Lifetime; its EMEA warranty document grants "PC Memory : 10 years" 23 — the spacing around the colon is as printed. Whether that document governs an Indian purchase was not established: its terms are EMEA terms, its body names no Indian entity or route, and PNY publishes no India warranty 23 12.

Second, and more practically, the route to a claim is the regional part. A term you cannot claim on is worth less than a shorter one you can, and the next section is about exactly that.

What none of the six does is publish a shorter term for India specifically. The variation that exists is by document and by region, not by a rule naming this country.

DeeperThe detail

Only one brand publishes a country-specific warranty term of any kind, and the country is not India. Patriot’s sits under a heading reading "Products purchased prior to January 2011": "For Russia, a lifetime warranty is defined as the period between ten (10) years from the original date of purchase by the end user" 20 — Russia, not India, and a legacy clause at that. Checked all six warranty documents: no India-specific term, extension or cap was found in any of them.

So the ordinary position is that an Indian buyer gets the brand’s global term. Our companion page on what a memory lifetime warranty actually promises sets out what those terms mean, and the short version is that they mean five different things: an industry norm, how long the part stays on sale, how long the company keeps making it, whatever the company later decides, and — in one case — nothing defined at all.

The channel exclusion is where India-specific risk actually lives, and it is covered in the section above. The second place is transferability. Kingston’s warranty "is non-transferable" 21 and Corsair’s is "limited to the original purchaser and is non-transferable, except where prohibited by applicable local law" 24. Patriot publishes no transferability clause at all — its cover simply runs to product "purchased directly from Patriot or through a Patriot authorized reseller" 20. In a market with an active second-hand trade in components, the two explicit clauses do real work: a used Kingston or Corsair module arrives with no warranty, whatever its condition.

ExpertFor specialists

One more thing bears on an Indian buyer and is easy to miss, because it appears in a statutory-rights clause rather than a warranty term.

Four of the six brands acknowledge that law sits above their published terms, but only two name any jurisdiction, and neither names India. Kingston’s clause, in capitals as printed, addresses "CITIZENS OF THE UK OR COUNTRIES THAT ARE MEMBERS OF THE EUROPEAN UNION" 21. Corsair names more — Switzerland, the United Kingdom, Québec and the United States — and quantifies a European floor: "In the European Union, the minimum warranty period provided by resellers is 2 years." 24 Patriot names no country at all, referring only to "warranty restriction laws in certain countries" 20.

What this page will and will not say about that. It records that no brand read here names Indian consumer law in its warranty. It does not tell you what rights Indian law gives you against a seller, because that is a legal question this site is not equipped to answer and no company document settles it. If a claim is refused and you think it should not have been, that is a question for a consumer forum or a lawyer, not for a hardware guide.

The honest summary for a buyer here. Your warranty in India is the brand’s global term, claimed through a route the brand mostly has not published, from a seller the brand mostly has not named, with a document you must keep. That is not a reason to avoid any of these brands — memory is reliable and most modules never fail. It is a reason to treat the lifetime warranty as a weaker promise here than the word suggests, and to spend the two minutes at purchase that make it claimable.

5.What happens when it fails

SimpleStart here

This is the part where being in India changes the answer most, and one brand’s solution is genuinely unusual.

Patriot, for Amazon purchases in India, does not want the module back at all. For regions "such as India, where direct product returns to Patriot Memory facilities present shipping difficulties", it asks for "Photographs of the product.", "Proof of purchase (e.g., Amazon order confirmation or invoice)." and — the striking one — "Proof of product destruction." It then states: "Once we receive and verify this documentation, Patriot Memory will process a refund for your purchase through Amazon." 25

So on that route the remedy is a refund, and the price of it is destroying the module and documenting that you did.

DeeperThe detail

Everywhere else, the question is where you post it, and the published answers are thin.

BrandWhere an Indian claim goes
KingstonAn India phone number, and a warranty that sends "all other Asian countries" back to the seller — no Indian address and no Kingston destination 26 21
Silicon PowerA Taiwan RMA address; its India entry is a heading and a generic link 22 11
PatriotAmazon buyers, the destruction route; others, "the Patriot location nearest them" 25 20
CorsairNo addresses at all; regions handled by rule 24
PNY, LexarNo India route found 12 15

Kingston’s is the clearest, and what it says about India is that Kingston is not the destination. It publishes real addresses for the United States, for Europe, the Middle East and Africa, and for Taiwan 21 — but its Asia procedure names only Taiwan and China, and then says: "In all other Asian countries, please contact your original point of purchase to obtain" warranty service 21. An Indian buyer is sent back to the shop. For India Kingston publishes a phone number — "1860-258-2255 Toll Call Monday to Saturday 09:30 - 17:00 GMT +5.5" 26 — which is a contact, not a destination. Silicon Power’s India entry on its locations page is a heading with a "Customer Service" link, and that link is not India-specific: Vietnam, the Philippines and Indonesia point at the identical generic form 11.

ExpertFor specialists

Patriot’s destruction requirement deserves a fair reading rather than treatment as an oddity, because it is a rational answer to a real problem — and because Patriot itself gives only one reason for it.

What Patriot says is that direct returns "present shipping difficulties" in those regions 25. It offers no cost rationale. The effect, which Patriot does not spell out, is to convert the remedy from a replacement into a refund, and to shift the effort from posting a package internationally to destroying a module and photographing it.

Two limits on it that a buyer should know. It is scoped to one sales channel: someone who bought the same module from an Indian retailer rather than Amazon is not covered by that article at all. And it sits against a flat statement in Patriot’s own warranty FAQ that "Refunds will not be provided for any product." 20 The likeliest reading is a general rule and a narrow exception — but Patriot publishes nothing saying so, and a buyer reading only the flat sentence would not know the exception existed.

Set against the alternatives, the destruction route is arguably the best deal on this page for an Indian buyer who qualifies for it. The alternatives are worse in different ways. Kingston sends an Indian buyer back to the retailer rather than to Kingston 21, which works only if the retailer is still trading and still cares. Where Kingston does take returns, the cost of getting the part there is the buyer’s: in its Europe, Middle East and Africa procedure, "You are responsible for the cost of insuring and shipping the product(s)" 21. PNY’s EMEA warranty — the only PNY document covering PC memory — says only that "Products must be sent to PNY carriage paid, in suitable packaging, by registered post with proof of delivery, and insured when appropriate" 23, and does not say who bears the cost of getting a package there from India. On a module worth a few thousand rupees, international postage plus duty can exceed the value of the part, which is the calculation that makes a lifetime warranty academic and is presumably what Patriot’s policy is responding to.

6.What Indian law actually requires

SimpleStart here

Three Indian rules touch a memory module, and the one most people expect is the one that does not apply.

  1. BIS registration: on the published schedule, not required for the module. India’s Compulsory Registration Scheme covers laptops, monitors, printers, keyboards, external drives and a general entry for "Automatic Data Processing Machine". No entry names a bare memory module 27. So there is no reason to expect a BIS registration number on retail RAM, and on the published schedule its absence is not a defect — the expert level sets out exactly what this rests on and what could not be checked.
  2. E-waste rules: these do reach it, as a component of a computer — but as an obligation on the brand owner or the importer, not as a mark on the box 28.
  3. The price on the package: required by law. Under the Legal Metrology packaging rules, a retail pack must declare "the retail sale price of the package" 29.
DeeperThe detail

On BIS, the detail matters because it is a negative claim. The Compulsory Registration Scheme is notified by the Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology, and BIS publishes the schedule of "Electronics and IT Goods under ‘Compulsory Registration Scheme’" 27. The computing entries include "Laptop/Notebook/Tablets", "Automatic Data Processing Machine", "Printers, Plotters", "Scanners", "Keyboard", "Wireless Keyboards", "Smart Card Readers", "Power Adaptors for IT Equipments", "USB Type External Hard Disk Drive" and "USB Type External Solid-State Storage Devices (above 256 GB capacity)" 27.

Read those last two beside each other, because the asymmetry is the finding. An external SSD above 256 GB needs BIS registration. An internal memory module does not appear anywhere on the list. The finished computer the module goes into is covered; the module is not.

On e-waste, the rules reach further than most buyers realise. The Central Pollution Control Board states that the 2022 rules apply to "Electrical & Electronic Equipment (EEE) including their components, consumables, parts and spares which make the product operational as listed in Schedule- I" 28. Schedule I lists "ITEW2 Personal Computing: Personal Computers (Central Processing Unit with input and output devices)" and "ITEW3 Personal Computing: Laptop Computers(Central Processing Unit with input and output devices)" 30 — the missing space before the bracket is the schedule’s. A memory module is a component that makes those operational, so extended producer responsibility falls on whoever is its "producer" in India — the brand owner or the importer, not the shop that sold it to you and not you, and not visible on the packaging.

The scale of that regime is worth knowing, because it is recent and large. The rules were notified on 2 November 2022 and took effect on 1 April 2023, extending extended producer responsibility from 21 categories to 106 items of electrical and electronic equipment, with recycling targets rising from "60% for the year 2023-2024 and 2024-25" to "70% for the year 2025-26 and 2026-27" and "80% for the year 2027-28 and 2028-29 and onwards" 31. The same rules require that products not contain hazardous substances beyond prescribed limits 31 — India’s equivalent of the RoHS restrictions several of these brands already advertise for Europe.

ExpertFor specialists

Three caveats on the BIS finding, because a negative regulatory claim deserves them.

  1. Two BIS sources disagree about which Order is in force. The BIS website states the schedule is notified under the "Electronics and Information Technology Goods (Requirement of Compulsory Registration) Order, 2021" 27, while the BIS-operated CRS portal still presents the 2012 Order as the applicable one and does not mention a 2021 Order at all 32; its companion page recites the 2012 Order and its expansions without asserting current applicability either way 33. No version contains a memory entry, so the conflict does not change the answer — but it is unresolved between two arms of the same body.
  2. The operative text of neither Order could be read for this check. The evidence is BIS’s own published schedule 27 plus the gazette notifications that could be read — S.O. 2742(E) of 2017 34, S.O. 1236(E) of 1 April 2020 35 and S.O. 3429(E) of 1 October 2020 36 — none of which contains a memory entry. The Orders themselves were served as scanned images without machine-readable text.
  3. Whether "Automatic Data Processing Machine" could sweep in a module is not settled by any source read here. No official definition of that term was found. The entry is tied to a standard whose published title is "Audio/Video, Information and Communication Technology Equipment – Part 1 Safety Requirements" 27 — an equipment-level electrical-safety standard. A bare module has no mains connection, no enclosure and no user-accessible energy source, so there is no coherent way to certify one against it. That is this page’s reasoning from the standard’s title, not a government scope ruling, and it is offered as such.

On packaging, the Legal Metrology rules require a pre-packaged commodity to declare "the name and address of the manufacturer, or where the manufacturer is not the packer, the name and address of the manufacturer and packer and for any imported package the name and address of the importer shall be mentioned on every package", "The common or generic names of the commodity contained in the package", "The net quantity, in terms of the standard unit of weight or measure", "The month and year in which the commodity is manufactured or pre-packed or imported", and "the retail sale price of the package" 29.

That last requirement is quietly the most useful thing on this page for a buyer. A legitimately imported, retail-packed module must carry an MRP and an importer’s name and address. A box with no MRP and no Indian importer named is a box that did not come through a channel that expected to be seen — which is the same question the warranty’s authorised-channel clause turns on, arriving from a different direction. This page does not claim that as a legal test; it notes that the two happen to point at the same thing.

7.Why this page shows no prices

SimpleStart here

You will not find a price comparison on this page, and the reason is a standing rule of this site rather than an oversight.

This site shows a price only where the company that makes the thing publishes one. Retailer prices are not used anywhere, in any section. They move weekly, they differ between sellers, they would be stale within days of writing, and repeating them would mean asserting something no manufacturer stands behind.

For memory in India that rule bites hard, because almost none of these companies publishes a price at all — and none publishes an Indian one. So the honest answer to “what should this cost?” is that this page cannot tell you, and any page that does is quoting a shop rather than a manufacturer.

DeeperThe detail

What each company does publish, from the individual guides in this section: Corsair publishes prices with a direct cart on its US store and none on the worldwide locale. PNY publishes US prices with its own cart, and its India storefront shows products with no prices at all 12. Kingston, Patriot, Lexar and Silicon Power publish no prices on their product pages in any region. The chip makers publish none for memory chips anywhere.

There is one price that Indian law does require, and it is the only officially-mandated one a buyer will see: the retail sale price declared on the package under the Legal Metrology rules 29. It is not a manufacturer’s recommendation in the sense the word usually implies — it is a maximum, declared by whoever packed or imported the goods. But it is a published figure with a legal basis behind it, which is more than a shop’s shelf price has.

What can be said about direction rather than level. Memory prices rose sharply through 2026 on the supply side, and the one element of that this page will state directly is the one with a first-party source: Micron announced its exit from the Crucial consumer business on 3 December 2025, with its EVP and Chief Business Officer Sumit Sadana saying the company "has made the difficult decision to exit the Crucial consumer business in order to improve supply and support for our larger, strategic customers in faster-growing segments" 5.

Two other figures circulate and this page does not assert them: a reported 45% DRAM wafer price rise at PSMC from July, and a roughly 100% quarter-on-quarter rise in Winbond’s blended ASPs in 2Q26. Neither could be confirmed against a first-party company publication in this check — the PSMC figure traces to press coverage of a briefing, and no Winbond publication stating the ASP figure was found. The PSMC and Winbond guides record what each company itself publishes.

ExpertFor specialists

The rule has a cost and it is worth being straight about it. A reader who wants to know whether ₹6,000 is a fair price for a 32 GB DDR5 kit this week will not get that here, and will have to look at retailers. This page is not a substitute for a price comparison and does not pretend to be.

What it offers instead is the part that does not go stale. Prices change weekly; warranty terms, authorised channels, corporate presence and regulatory status change over years. Those are the things a buyer cannot easily check and that no retailer will tell them, and they are what decides whether a cheap module is a bargain or a module with no recourse behind it.

One caution about Indian price comparison specifically, since this is where the two halves meet. A price well below the market is frequently a parallel import, and the section on who you are allowed to buy from explains what that costs you: the part is genuine, and the warranty is the thing you are actually giving up. That trade may well be worth making — on a component that rarely fails, a large discount against a warranty you would probably never claim is a reasonable bet, and this page does not tell anyone not to make it. It is only worth making knowingly.

And the structural reason prices are unusually unstable here, for context rather than prediction: the three companies that make most of the world’s DRAM sell the same wafers into data centres and into consumer modules, and through 2026 the data-centre side paid better. Sumit Sadana, Micron’s EVP and Chief Business Officer, put it plainly when the company left the consumer business: "The AI-driven growth in the data center has led to a surge in demand for memory and storage." 5 Consumer memory pricing in India is downstream of that, and no Indian factor — duty, GST, distribution — is the main driver.

8.What to check before you buy

SimpleStart here

Five things, in the order they matter, and all of them take about two minutes at the point of purchase.

  1. Get a GST invoice in your name, naming the brand and the part number. Every warranty read for this site requires proof of purchase, and some require it to be from an authorised seller.
  2. Check the box carries an MRP and an importer’s name and address. Indian packaging rules require both on a retail pack 29. A retail-packed box with neither did not arrive through a channel expecting to be seen — bulk and OEM modules sold loose are a separate case.
  3. Buy the whole capacity in one kit. Two of the six — Kingston and Patriot — require the entire kit back for a warranty claim 21 20, and a second kit bought later is a separate purchase with separate proof.
  4. If the brand publishes an Indian channel list, use it. Patriot and Silicon Power do 10 19. The others do not, which is itself worth knowing.
  5. Do not remove the heat spreader. Patriot excludes "Products that are missing the Patriot label, and or module heat spreaders" 20 — the "and or" is Patriot’s.
DeeperThe detail

Two of those deserve expanding, because they are the ones where Indian buyers most often lose the argument later.

The invoice. It is not enough that you have proof you paid; the document should identify what you bought. A warranty claim two years from now turns on establishing that this module was that purchase, from that seller, on that date. A GST invoice naming brand and part number does all three. A payment screenshot does none of them.

The kit rule catches people who are being sensible about money. Buying 16 GB now and 16 GB later is the natural way to spread a build across two paydays, and it is the one thing every brand that addresses the subject advises against — not only because the modules may not match, but because Kingston and Patriot both require the whole kit back on a claim 21 20. Two 16 GB kits bought six months apart are two separate warranty positions, and if one module fails you may be returning a pair you did not buy together. Our page on whose DRAM chips are inside your memory stick explains why the chips themselves may differ.

And one thing not to worry about: the absence of a BIS registration number on a memory module is expected. No entry on the published compulsory-registration schedule names one 27, so its absence tells you nothing about whether the part is genuine or properly imported — though the operative text of the Order itself could not be read for this check. The MRP and the importer’s details are the packaging marks that carry information; a missing BIS number is not one.

ExpertFor specialists

What could not be established for this page, stated plainly, because the gaps are as useful as the findings.

  1. Whether PNY’s EMEA warranty governs an Indian purchase. Its terms are EMEA terms, its body names no Indian entity or route, and PNY publishes no India warranty 23 12. So whether an Indian PNY buyer gets the ten years that document states is not established here, and PNY is the brand a buyer would most want an answer from.
  2. Where an Indian buyer sends a Corsair, Lexar or PNY module. None publishes an Indian route 24 15 12.
  3. Whether Silicon Power has any India-specific service path. Its locations page gives a heading and a link shared with three other countries, and that link could not be retrieved 11.
  4. What Crucial’s Indian terms are. Crucial’s site could not be retrieved for this check in any region, so nothing on this site describes them.
  5. Whether the operative text of either CRS Order contains a clause sweeping in components of listed goods. Neither Order could be read; see the previous section for exactly what the negative claim does and does not rest on.

A closing note on what this page is for. Indian buyers are well served on price by a competitive retail market and badly served on everything else, because the questions that decide whether a purchase goes wrong — who stands behind it, where it goes when it fails, whether the seller is inside the brand’s channel — are answered in documents written for other countries and rarely mentioning this one. This page collects what the companies and the government actually publish, and is equally clear about what neither does. Where it says something could not be established, that is a limit of the published record and of this check, not a claim about what is true.
