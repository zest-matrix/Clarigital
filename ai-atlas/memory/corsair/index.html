Corsair: The Complete Guide

Corsair does not make a single memory chip, and it tells the United States securities regulator exactly whose chips it buys. DRAM was just under a third of its revenue last year. It also publishes something almost nobody else does: a code on the sticker that reveals which chip maker is inside the stick you already own. Three reading levels, every number sourced.

1.At a glance

SimpleStart here

Corsair makes memory modules, not memory chips. It buys the chips from the companies documented earlier in this section, puts them on its own circuit boards, sorts and tests them, and sells them under its own name 1.

What it makesDDR5 and DDR4 modules, plus power supplies, cooling, cases, prebuilt PCs, keyboards, mice, headsets and sim-racing wheels 2
How big$1.47bn of net revenue in 2025, of which DRAM modules were 32.3% 3 2
Whose chipsSamsung, Micron and Hynix — Corsair names all three in its annual report 2
Any HBM?None appears anywhere in its annual report’s product descriptions, and it runs no fab — it buys all its DRAM chips in 2
DeeperThe detail

Corsair is the second module brand in this section, after Kingston, and it is a different kind of company in one way that changes everything about what can be checked. Kingston is private and publishes no accounts. Corsair is listed on Nasdaq and therefore must file them 2 4. That means the interesting questions about a module brand — how much of the business is really memory, whose chips go inside, what happens when chip prices move — are not matters of marketing copy here. They are answered in a document a regulator can hold the company to.

The answers are unusually direct. DRAM modules were 32.3% of net revenue in 2025 and 29.2% in 2024 2. The chips come from three named companies. And the cost structure is stated plainly: "Integrated circuits, or ICs, account for most of the cost of producing our high-performance memory products" 5.

Corsair is also much more than a memory brand, which Kingston is not. Memory sits inside one of two reporting segments alongside power supplies, cooling, cases and whole PCs, while the other segment sells keyboards, mice, headsets, stream decks and steering wheels 2. A reader who thinks of Corsair as a RAM company is looking at about a third of it.

ExpertFor specialists

One framing point worth getting right before reading further, because Corsair's own disclosures invite the error. The company reports two segments — Gamer and Creator Peripherals, and Gaming Components and Systems — and memory lives inside the second 2. Separately, its quarterly filings also break revenue down by product category, where "Memory products" appears as its own line 6. That line is a disaggregation for disclosure purposes, not a reportable segment. Quoting "Memory products" revenue as though it were segment revenue mixes two different cuts of the same business, and the two are not interchangeable.

The second point is about where the growth currently is. In the quarter ended 30 June 2026, Memory products revenue was $123.0m against $75.4m for all other component products, and the other-components line had fallen from $112.5m a year earlier while memory rose from $105.0m 6. Memory was about 39% of the company's $314.3m of net revenue that quarter — our arithmetic on the filing's own figures — and the only component category growing at all.

DeeperThe detail

Corsair is a controlled company in the technical Nasdaq sense, and it says so itself. Its 2026 proxy states that EagleTree "owns approximately 53% of our combined voting power as of April 20, 2026" and that "Due to EagleTree holding more than 50% of the voting power for the election of directors, we are considered a 'controlled company' for the purposes of the Nasdaq Rules" 10. Its annual report lists the consequence as a risk factor in its own words: the company "qualif[ies] for, and intend[s] to rely on, exemptions from certain corporate governance requirements", and "You will not have the same protections afforded to stockholders of companies that are subject to such requirements" 2.

The corporate group is larger than the brand suggests. Exhibit 21.1 to the 2025 annual report lists 23 subsidiaries across Australia, Canada, the Cayman Islands, China, France, Germany, Hong Kong, Japan, the Netherlands, Singapore, Slovenia, Taiwan, the United Kingdom, the United States and Vietnam 11. Two are worth noticing by name: Corsair Memory, Inc. in Delaware and Corsair Memory Co., Ltd in Taiwan — the memory business has its own legal entities, and one of them is Taiwanese, where the modules are assembled 11 2.

Corsair is a JEDEC member, listed as Corsair Memory Inc. on JEDEC's own member roster 12 — the same standards body whose DDR5 specification its own explainers point readers to 13 14.

ExpertFor specialists

The board composition follows from the control position. The 2026 proxy lists nine directors: George L. Majoros, Jr. as chair, chief executive Thi La, Jason Glen Cahilly, Samuel R. Szteinbaum, Anup Bagaria, Stuart A. Martin, Randall J. Weisenburger, Diana Bell and Sarah Mears Kim 10. Majoros and Bagaria also appear as reporting persons on the Schedule 13D filed for the EagleTree holding vehicles 15, which names the chain as Corsair Group (Cayman), LP; EagleTree-Carbide (GP), LLC; EagleTree Partners IV (GP), LP; and EagleTree Partners IV Ultimate GP, LLC 15.

Two leadership changes landed inside twelve months, which is worth knowing when reading commentary from either period. Founder Andy Paul's retirement was announced on 12 February 2025 and took effect on 1 July 2025, when Thi La — previously chief operating officer since 2013 and president since 2021 — became chief executive; Paul left the board at the same time, not merely the executive role 16 8. The finance role turned over in November 2025: Michael G. Potter resigned as chief financial officer effective 2 December 2025 — the same day Gordon Mattingly was appointed — then left the company on 31 December 2025 and provided transitional services under a consulting arrangement from 1 January to 31 March 2026 9. Any earnings-call transcript from that window can therefore carry a speaker attribution that looks wrong and is merely stale.

3.The story

SimpleStart here

Corsair is older than most of the brands on a modern motherboard, and it did not start out selling memory to gamers.

  • 1994 "We were originally incorporated in 1994" 17. As Corsair puts it: "Andy Paul and three other engineers founded Corsair in Silicon Valley in 1994" 16.
  • 1998 Corsair "began selling branded high-performance gaming PC memory" — four years after incorporation 17.
  • 2011 Its Taiwan production facility is certified to ISO 9001:2008 for "testing, assembly and packaging of Corsair's DRAM products" 18.
  • 2017 The investment firm EagleTree Capital buys the business "for aggregate consideration of approximately $550 million" 17.
  • 2018 The acquisition vehicle, incorporated in Delaware in 2017 as EagleTree-Carbide Acquisition Corp., is renamed Corsair Gaming, Inc. 2.
  • 2020 Nasdaq listing: 14,000,000 shares at "$17.00 per share" 4.
  • 2021 First DDR5 modules. Corsair notes that "DDR5 modules are the first equipped with onboard voltage regulation" 19.
  • 2024 Buys the Fanatec sim-racing business out of Endor AG 20.
  • 2025 Founder Andy Paul retires after 31 years; Thi La becomes chief executive 16 8.
  • 2026 Launches SHUGO, a "collectible memory series" 21.
DeeperThe detail

The four-year gap between 1994 and 1998 is the most interesting thing in Corsair's own account of itself, precisely because Corsair does not explain it. Its prospectus says the company "was originally incorporated in 1994 and began selling branded high-performance gaming PC memory in 1998" 17, and its annual report says only "Since our founding in 1994, we have shipped more than 320 million units of gaming and creator products as of December 31, 2025" and "We have served the market for three decades" 2. What the business did between 1994 and 1998 is not stated in any Corsair or SEC document retrieved for this check. Widely repeated accounts exist, but they rest on tertiary sources, so this page does not carry them.

The same caution applies to the founders. Corsair’s own retirement release says "Andy Paul and three other engineers founded Corsair in Silicon Valley in 1994" — four founders — but it does not name the other three 16. Tertiary sources name three, which contradicts Corsair's own count, so no names appear here.

Corsair has bought a great deal since going private and then public again. Its annual report counts "the ten acquisitions we have completed since 2018, including the Fanatec acquisition in September 2024" 2, and the brands it now lists are Corsair, Elgato, Origin, SCUF, Drop and Fanatec 2. Of the individual deals, Elgato's gaming division was agreed in June 2018 22, ORIGIN PC in July 2019 23, SCUF Gaming in December 2019 24, and a 51% — majority, not whole — stake in the Taiwanese display designer iDisplay in January 2022 25. None of those releases discloses a price.

ExpertFor specialists

The Fanatec transaction is the one where the filing and the press release describe genuinely different things, and the filing is the more precise document. Corsair's press release said it had acquired "the Fanatec Sim Racing brand and all associated personnel, in an asset purchase from Endor AG" and that "Financial terms of the acquisition were not disclosed" 20. Its quarterly filing describes the same deal as "the business (the 'Fanatec Business') of Endor AG ('Endor'), which includes certain assets and all the personnel of Endor, as well as the equity interests of certain of Endor's subsidiaries", closing on 19 September 2024, for approximately $43.7 million net of $4.5 million of cash acquired 26.

So the price was disclosed — in the accounts, not the announcement. The filing also reveals the shape of the deal: Corsair had entered "a Bridge Loan Agreement ('Bridge Loan') in May 2024 and a Debtor-In-Possession Loan Agreement ('DIP Loan') in September 2024 with Endor to provide short-term financing for Endor's operations", and those loans, totalling about $16.6 million, were set off against the purchase price at closing 26. Corsair lent the target money, then deducted the loans from what it paid for it. Note also what it did not buy: the filing's careful "certain assets" and "certain of Endor's subsidiaries" means Corsair did not acquire Endor AG, the listed company 26. Exhibit 21.1 shows the pieces that did come across — Corsair Fanatec Australia PTY Ltd., Corsair Fanatec Japan KK, Endor Technology (Shenzhen) Co., Ltd. and Endor Hong Kong Ltd. 11.

On ownership, the drift from EagleTree's original position to today's is a dilution story rather than a selling story. The Schedule 13D of November 2022 reports EagleTree’s holding as "56,300,771 shares", or "56.0%" 15. That same share count still appears in filings years later, against a share count that has grown — 106,662,337 shares outstanding at 12 February 2026 2 and 108,176,959 at 28 July 2026 27. 56,300,771 against roughly 107 million is about 53%, which is what the 2026 proxy says 10. EagleTree's holding fell as a percentage because the company issued more shares, not because EagleTree sold.

Speeds, as Corsair lists them on its own memory landing page in a column headed "MT/SEC", top out at 8,000 for Dominator Titanium, 7,600 for Vengeance RGB and Vengeance, 4,800 for the DDR5 SODIMM and 4,000 for the DDR4 SODIMM 32. Those are family ceilings for ordinary modules. The genuinely fast parts are a different form factor — see below.

The fastest kit found listed for sale in this check is a CUDIMM: Vengeance 48GB (2x24GB) DDR5 CUDIMM at 9200 MT/s, part number CMKC48GX5M2X9200C44, with tested latency 44-56-56-148 at 1.45V against an SPD default of 5600 MHz and 48-48-48-90 at 1.1V 33. The CUDIMM listings seen in this check ran 7000, 8000, 8400, 8800 and 9200 MT/s — but see the caution that follows. A caution on that "fastest" claim: Corsair's DDR5 category pages are script-rendered with "load more" pagination and their sort controls did not reorder results, so the catalogue could not be exhaustively enumerated in this check. A faster listed part cannot be ruled out.

Corsair also publishes a hardware tier marker most brands do not. Its memory hub shows an "IC Performance Bin" row on product cards, reading "Plus" for Dominator Titanium and "Standard" for Vengeance and Vengeance RGB 34 35. A related field, "PMIC Type", reads "Overclock PMIC" on a 6000 MT/s Vengeance RGB kit and "Extreme OC PMIC" on the 7200 MT/s Dominator Platinum RGB and the 9200 MT/s CUDIMM 36 37 33. These are published, comparable fields rather than adjectives.

ExpertFor specialists

Two lines deserve separating out because they are not consumer memory at all. WS DDR5 ECC RDIMM targets Intel Xeon W-3400/W-2400 on W790 and AMD Threadripper PRO 7000/WX on TRX50 and WRX90, for "memory hungry applications such as 3D imaging, video applications, and CAD simulations", from DDR5-4800 base up to a tested DDR5-6400 and 64–256GB, carrying both "Intel XMP 3.0 support" and "AMD EXPO support" 31. The published part CMA64GX5M4B6400C32 is a 64GB four-module kit at 6400 MT/s CL32 and 1.35V, requiring "Intel W790 or newer" 38. Registered ECC memory in a gaming brand's catalogue is unusual and easy to miss.

SHUGO, announced 26 May 2026, is the newest line and the strangest. Corsair calls it a "limited-edition enthusiast memory series created for builders who see the machine as both a performance PC and a personal design statement", built with "hand-selected, highly binned DDR5 ICs" and a "Patent-pending light-emitting micro-drilling technology, which allows RGB lighting to shine through the side of the module" 21. The published parts are 6000 MT/s CL28 32GB kits under the names ONYX BLADE (CMZ32GX5M2X6000Z28OB) and SAKURA NOA (CMZ32GX5M2X6000Z28SN), with a UV-printed aluminium heat spreader 28.

And one line that does not exist yet. Corsair publishes a full explainer on CAMM2 and LPCAMM2, describing CAMM2 as "Compression Attached Memory Module (version two)" with contacts "arranged in a grid, similar to what you'd find on a modern processor", and calling LPCAMM2 "a much more attractive option" for laptops 39. That page was read in full and appeared complete, and it announces no Corsair CAMM2 or LPCAMM2 product — its only forward-looking line is "rest assured that CORSAIR will ensure you have access to the best memory solutions to maximize your system's performance" 39. No such part was found in any catalogue page in this check.

5.How a module is built

SimpleStart here

A module brand's whole job sits in four steps, and Corsair describes each of them in its own words.

  1. Buy the chips. "We use DRAM ICs produced by Samsung, Micron and Hynix in our DRAM modules. We purchase those DRAM ICs, pursuant to purchase orders and not long-term supply contracts, largely from third-party distributors and, to a lesser extent, directly from those manufacturers" 2.
  2. Sort them. Modules are built with "hand-sorted, tightly-screened memory chips to ensure consistent high-frequency performance" 19. The top line uses "hand-selected, highly binned DDR5 ICs, chosen for their ability to maintain stability at high frequencies and aggressive timings" 28.
  3. Put them on a Corsair board. Product pages list a "CUSTOM HIGH-PERFORMANCE PCB" 37; the DDR4 Vengeance LPX uses an "eight-layer PCB" that "helps manage heat and provides superior overclocking headroom" 40.
  4. Cool it and test it. DHX — "Dual-path Heat eXchange" — "lets heat dissipate from the memory both vertically through fins and horizontally through the printed circuit board (PCB)" 1, and kits are "all hand-tested for consistently high clock speeds and stable operation" 1.
DeeperThe detail

The sorting language is worth reading closely, because Corsair uses it consistently and it is the actual product. The recurring phrase across press releases and product pages is "hand-sorted, tightly-screened memory chips" 19 30 36 37, and the Dominator Titanium release the same phrase without the second hyphen 41. Cheaper lines get a softer version — "Tightly screened memory ICs picked for their reliability and tuning headroom" 29 — and the workstation line a different one again: "ECC modules are screened and selected carefully to deliver the best performance" 31. Corsair’s own DDR5 FAQ makes the generational point directly: "DDR5 can also run at higher clock frequencies than DDR4; the latter requires very careful binning in order to reach a similar speed rating" 42 — reaching a given speed on the older generation is a sorting problem.

Put the sorting language next to the supply disclosure and the business model is visible. Corsair buys chips from three different makers, largely through distributors rather than direct 2, then sorts and tests the result so that the buyer does not have to care which maker's chips arrived. That is what the brand sells. It is the same proposition as Kingston's, reached by a slightly different route — Kingston qualifies its suppliers and tests every module; Corsair sorts incoming chips into performance bins and publishes the bin.

The cooling design is the part Corsair claims as its own invention. DHX is described as "patented CORSAIR DHX cooling design" 19, "patented CORSAIR DHX cooling technology for improved overclocking potential" 41, and on the Dominator Platinum RGB product page simply as the feature heading "PATENTED CORSAIR DHX COOLING" 37. The Titanium landing page puts it most plainly: "Our DHX technology uses a custom PCB to cool the DRAM chips and PCB for optimal performance" 35.

ExpertFor specialists

A limit of this page, stated rather than glossed. Corsair publishes the vocabulary of binning but not its thresholds. "Hand-sorted", "tightly-screened", "hand-selected, highly binned" and the published "IC Performance Bin" values of "Plus" and "Standard" 34 are all real and all Corsair's own 19 28, but no document retrieved in this check states what test a chip must pass to earn "Plus" rather than "Standard", who performs the sort, or on what equipment. That is not the same as saying Corsair publishes nothing about it — only that the pages read here, listed in the sources below, do not say.

What Corsair does put a number on is the electrical design. In Corsair's words, "DDR5 modules are the first equipped with onboard voltage regulation" 19 — a statement about the generation rather than about Corsair — the regulator being the module's PMIC, whose grade Corsair publishes per part as "Overclock PMIC" or "Extreme OC PMIC" 36 33. That regulator is also what iCUE reaches when it adjusts module voltage: "You can use iCUE to regulate the memory module's voltage for more stable overclocking" 42. On DDR4 there was no such thing — voltage regulation lived on the motherboard — which is why the DDR4 and DDR5 halves of Corsair's own catalogue read so differently.

DeeperThe detail

The sentence that matters is "nearly all of our DRAM modules" 2. Corsair does not outsource module assembly the way it outsources cases and headsets — memory is made in-house, and the Taiwanese legal entity that does it has its own name in the subsidiary list: Corsair Memory Co., Ltd 11. For a brand often described as an assembler of other people's work, memory is the part it actually builds.

Where in Taiwan is harder to pin down, and the honest answer involves a date. The only Corsair document found in this check that names a city for its DRAM facility is a press release from 23 February 2011, announcing ISO 9001:2008 certification for a facility in "TAO YUAN, Taiwan" used for "testing, assembly and packaging of Corsair's DRAM products", described as "40,000 square foot" with "200 employees" 18. That release predates DDR4 entirely, let alone DDR5, so it should not be read as a description of where current kits are made — only as evidence that Corsair has published a DRAM plant location once, and where it was then.

The careers page, as retrieved, names offices in Milpitas, Duluth and San Diego; Miami; Cheltenham, St Albans and Wokingham; Almere; Ljubljana; Aachen, Landshut, Munich and Mönchengladbach; Taipei and Taoyuan; Shenzhen; Hong Kong; and Ho Chi Minh City 43. Taoyuan appears there too, which is consistent with the 2011 release, though a careers listing is not a statement about manufacturing.

Every attempt at the filing — six by a research pass, and one made directly for this page — truncated inside Item 1A, Risk Factors; the furthest any of them reached was Item 1C, Cybersecurity, with Item 2 beginning on page 39 2. What could be read is the risk language about concentration, and it is blunter than the marketing. Alongside the Taiwan, China and Vietnam reliance 2, the supply-chain risk factor states that "We and our manufacturers rely on an extended third-party supply chain and the availability of raw materials to produce a significant amount of our products", and that "From time to time, we have experienced product shortages due to both disruptions in supply from the third parties that manufacture or supply our products and our inability or the inability of these third-party manufacturers to obtain necessary components, and we may experience similar shortages in the future" 2. The trade-policy risk factor adds exposure to "changes in trade relations or the implementation of tariffs or economic and trade sanctions and export restrictions, in particular any tariffs, sanctions or export restrictions affecting trade between the United States and China" 2.

7.How the memory works

SimpleStart here

Two things about a modern Corsair module are genuinely new, and both have names worth knowing.

The module regulates its own voltage. On DDR4 the motherboard fed the memory its power. On DDR5 a chip on the stick itself — the PMIC — does it, and Corsair puts the generational point plainly: "DDR5 modules are the first equipped with onboard voltage regulation now conveniently controlled through CORSAIR iCUE software" 19.

Some modules carry their own clock. A CUDIMM is a "Clocked Unbuffered Dual In-line Memory Module" 13. It adds a clock driver that, in Corsair's words, "reconditions both the timing and voltage amplitude of these signals, enhancing signal integrity by reducing clock jitter" and ensures "each chip on the module gets the same precise timing information" 44. That is how a stick reaches speeds like 9200 MT/s 33.

And one thing that has not changed: memory ships slow and is told to go fast. A kit sold as 6000 MT/s starts at a slower default until a profile is switched on in the BIOS — which is what XMP and EXPO are for 45.

DeeperThe detail

The default-versus-rated gap is printed on Corsair's own spec tables and is the single most misunderstood thing about buying memory. The 9200 MT/s CUDIMM lists a "Speed Rating" of PC5-73600 (DDR5-9200) with a tested latency of 44-56-56-148 at 1.45V — and, on the same page, an SPD speed of 5600 MHz with latency 48-48-48-90 at 1.1V 33. SPD is what the module reports to the machine when it boots. Until a profile is enabled, that 9200 MT/s kit runs at 5600.

Corsair defines the two profile standards itself: "Intel XMP (eXtreme Memory Profile) and AMD EXPO (Extended Profiles for Overclocking) are two technologies that make it simple to run your memory at its optimal settings", with XMP "specific to Intel platforms" and EXPO "supported by AMD's latest DDR5-ready AM5 processors and motherboards" 45. The generation mapping is in its FAQs: "DDR5 will come with XMP 3.0" 42, while "DDR4 employs a new specification, XMP 2.0" 46.

CUDIMMs carry a platform catch that Corsair states plainly and buyers routinely miss. They are "fully compatible with Intel platforms", but AMD "doesn't support the Single or Dual PLL modes yet", so AMD systems run a CUDIMM in Bypass mode, where "The CKD doesn't regenerate and amplify the signal; basically, it works as a traditional UDIMM" and, in Corsair’s words, "speeds may be limited to 6,000MT/s" on older DDR5 platforms 44. The 9200 MT/s kit's own compatibility field says "Intel 800 Series" and its performance profile is Intel XMP alone 33. A 9200 MT/s CUDIMM in an AMD machine is not a 9200 MT/s kit.

ExpertFor specialists

On dual profiles, Corsair changed its position and dated the change, which is useful. Its explainer records an update of 20 January 2026 stating that "all CORSAIR AMD Memory Kits are now dual profile, meaning that they are compatible with EXPO and XMP", and — the asymmetry that matters — "Intel Memory kits are not dual-profile" 47. Earlier the dual-profile scope was narrower: "select AMD EXPO DDR5 memory kits…specifically those kits that are rated at 6,000 MT/s or slower with CAS latencies of 30 or higher" 47. EXPO first arrived on Corsair DDR5 on 30 August 2022, covering the "for AMD" variants of Dominator Platinum RGB, Vengeance RGB and Vengeance at speeds up to 6,000 MT/s 48.

The live catalogue bears this out and shows how to read a part number for it. Corsair’s own decoder gives the pairing directly, using the two Dominator parts as its worked examples: "CMP32GX5M2X7200C34 = Intel XMP" and "CMP32GX5M2B6000Z30 = AMD EXPO" 49. The catalogue matches — the AMD Dominator Titanium RGB parts carry Z, listed as CMP32GX5M2B6000Z30 and CMP32GX5M2B6000Z30W 35, while the Intel-only high-speed parts use C: CMT32GX5M2X7200C34, CMKC48GX5M2X9200C44, CMH32GX5M2E6000C36 37 33 36. Corsair's own decoder confirms the convention: DDR5 XMP kits take a "C__" latency suffix and AMD EXPO kits a "Z__" suffix 49.

A word on the software, because it is where Corsair's memory differs most from a generic module. iCUE reaches the module itself: through it a user can "customize and synchronize the lighting effects", "see the current temperature and voltage readings of each memory module" in the Cooling tab, set temperature-triggered actions in the Alerts tab, and "create custom XMP profiles to tailor performance for specific apps and tasks" 50. Reading a DIMM's own temperature and rewriting its XMP profile from the desktop are things DDR5's on-module electronics made possible and that Corsair chose to expose.

Whether the memory needs iCUE is a fair question and the honest answer is partial. Corsair documents that lighting persists without it: Hardware Lighting is "an iCUE feature you can use to set Lighting Effects to display whenever iCUE is not running", including on "a computer that does not have iCUE installed", and DRAM is named among the products whose profiles are "saved automatically" 51. Its cheaper RGB line can also be driven by motherboard software instead — ASUS Aura Sync, MSI Mystic Light or Gigabyte RGB Fusion 29. An explicit statement that the modules themselves run normally without iCUE was not found on the pages read for this check; what is documented is that the lighting does, and that the monitoring and profile-authoring features are what require the software 50 51.

One standards footnote. Corsair's form-factor explainer points readers to JEDEC for DDR5 behind the link text "JEDEC specifications", whose target is the JESD79-5C01 page — Corsair does not name the revision in visible text 13. That URL now serves JESD79-5D, version 1.41, published November 2025 14, so the revision Corsair linked has been superseded. Worth knowing if a specification argument turns on which revision is being quoted.

8.Specs explained

SimpleStart here

Corsair part numbers look like noise and are not. Its own worked example decodes CMK32GX4M2Z3200C16W 49:

PieceCorsair's own explanation
CM"Corsair Memory"
K"The memory kit line; in this case, it is part of the LPX line"
32G"The total capacity of the memory kit and the sum of all modules' capacities"
X4"The DDR generation of the memory kit; in this case, it is DDR4"

So the letter after CM tells you the family. Corsair's published list for DDR5: K Vengeance, H Vengeance RGB, T Dominator Platinum RGB, P Dominator Titanium RGB; on DDR4, W Vengeance RGB PRO and G Vengeance RGB RS 52.

One revision convention is easy to trip over. In the DDR5 part number the letter before the speed is a model revision: Corsair states that DDR5 revisions A through E mean "1.435V on VCC/VDD" while X means "Extrem OC with 1.9V on VCC/VDD" — its spelling — and on DDR4 a Z means "Ryzen optimisation for DDR4" 52. The X in CMT32GX5M2X7200C34 is therefore not decoration 37.

ExpertFor specialists

Corsair's published decoder has fallen behind its own catalogue, and anyone relying on it should know where. It is not a thin page: it lists thirteen DDR4 family letters and five DDR5 ones, running from Value Select and Vengeance LED through to Dominator Titanium RGB, and it covers SODIMMs on both generations 52. But three prefixes visibly in use on live parts are missing from it: CMKC and CMHC for CUDIMMs 44 33, CMZ for SHUGO 28, and CMA for the WS ECC RDIMM line 38. The decoder is not wrong about what it covers; it is simply older than the catalogue. A newer or localised decoder may exist that this check did not find.

Two smaller conventions from the support article, both useful and neither obvious: "If a DDR5 kit has a K at the end, this means it a revised kit", and the latency-suffix rule separating XMP (C) from EXPO (Z) kits 49. On the kit-versus-module question, note that capacity in the part number is the kit total, not the module size — "the sum of all modules' capacities" 49 — so CMKC48GX5M2X9200C44 is two 24GB modules, not two 48GB ones 33.

Two qualifications keep that finding honest. The first is where the prices are. On Corsair's worldwide catalogue locale the same Vengeance kit's purchase button reads "SHOP NOW" rather than "Add to cart", and no price appears in the page body 55. The second is that prices on a live store are not a published price list. Any figure in the table above is a snapshot taken in September 2026, not a recommended price.

Corsair's press releases, for their part, generally decline to give a price and point at the store instead — "For up-to-date pricing…please refer to the CORSAIR website" 41. There is at least one historical exception: the 2017 release for its then-fastest DDR4 kit published an MSRP of "$549.99 Exc. Tax" for CMK16GX4M2F4600C19 57.

ExpertFor specialists

What Corsair publishes instead of a price list is a warranty schedule, and on that schedule memory is the best-covered category it sells. DRAM modules carry a "Limited Lifetime" warranty, against a general floor where "All new Corsair branded products include at least a 2-year warranty" 58. For contrast, the same schedule gives SSDs three to five years, flash devices five to ten, power supplies three to ten, cases two, cooling two to six, gaming peripherals two, graphics cards two and certified refurbished units ninety days 58. Memory is the only line at lifetime cover. The product pages carry the same badge — "LIMITED LIFETIME WARRANTY — For complete peace of mind and years of worry-free performance" 33 — and the workstation line adds "24/7 technical support assistance" 31.

That is the same trade Kingston makes, and it is worth naming as a pattern rather than a coincidence: a module brand that cannot control the price of its main input publishes a warranty instead of a price, because the warranty is the thing it can actually promise. Corsair's filings explain why the input price is beyond its control — "The market for these ICs is highly competitive and cyclical", and "Declines in the market price of ICs enable our competitors to lower prices and we will likely be forced to lower our product prices in order to compete effectively" 2.

10.Whose chips are inside

SimpleStart here

Samsung, Micron and Hynix. Corsair does not leave this to a blog post — it states it in the annual report it files with the United States securities regulator:

"We use DRAM ICs produced by Samsung, Micron and Hynix in our DRAM modules. We purchase those DRAM ICs, pursuant to purchase orders and not long-term supply contracts, largely from third-party distributors and, to a lesser extent, directly from those manufacturers." 2

And Corsair goes one step further than any brand in this section so far. It publishes how to tell which one is in a stick you already own. On the module's information sticker there is a version number, and Corsair maps it 53:

Version on the stickerWhose chips
V3.XX"Micron-based ICs" 53
V4.XX"Samsung-based ICs" 53
V5.XX"Hynix-based ICs" 53

Corsair tells you where to look: "look at the version number printed on the information sticker" 53.

DeeperThe detail

Now the sentence that makes this section genuinely interesting, and it is also Corsair's own: "CORSAIR memory kits may be built with different integrated circuits (ICs) even if the kits share the same part number" 53.

Read the two disclosures together and the picture is precise. Corsair names its three chip suppliers 2. It publishes a code that identifies which of the three is inside a given module 53. But it does not tie a chip maker to a part number, which means the version code is something a buyer can read after the box is open, not something they can specify before ordering. The same catalogue number can arrive with Micron, Samsung or Hynix chips inside, and Corsair says so.

The version code carries a second layer, which the decoder page adds: "The sub version relates to the IC manufacturer revision or PCB revision" 52. So V4.31 and V4.32 are both Samsung-based but not necessarily identical parts. The same page confirms the mapping holds "For DDR3, DDR4, and DDR5" 52 and notes "You could find version of your memory written on the memory itself" 52.

This matters practically to exactly one group of people and matters a great deal to them: anyone mixing a new kit with an existing one, or replacing a single failed module. Two kits with the same part number may not contain the same silicon.

ExpertFor specialists

Corsair has named a specific die once, in a press release, and it is worth recording because it shows the company will be specific when the specificity is a selling point. Announcing its fastest DDR4 kit on 14 September 2017 — CMK16GX4M2F4600C19, 4,600 MHz at CL19-26-26-46 and 1.5V — Corsair said it was "Built using hand-sorted industry-leading Samsung B-die IC's" 57. That is a named maker and a named die revision, in Corsair's own words.

What could not be established, stated as a limit rather than a finding. Across the pages read in full for this check — the IC article, the version decoder, both RAM FAQs, the Memory Explained explainer, the memory hub and landing pages, and the individual Vengeance, Vengeance RGB, Dominator Platinum RGB, Dominator Titanium RGB, Titanium First Edition, CUDIMM 9200, Vengeance LPX and WS RDIMM product pages — no per-part-number die-vendor lookup was found, and no current DDR5 product page names a die maker in its specifications. Nor was any statement found either promising or refusing to confirm a vendor on request. Note that a mirror of the IC article on Corsair's support site could not be read in this check, and it may say more. So: Corsair names its suppliers and publishes how to identify them after purchase; whether it will tell a buyer in advance is not something this check could determine either way.

On HBM — the section heading a chip maker would see here — there is nothing to report and that is the point. Corsair runs no wafer fab — its annual report has it assembling, testing and packaging modules in Taiwan from bought-in chips 2 — and neither "HBM" nor "high-bandwidth memory" appears anywhere in that filing or on any Corsair memory page read for this check. Its exposure to the HBM boom is entirely indirect, and entirely as a victim of it rather than a participant: the same AI build-out that consumes HBM capacity is what its filings blame for the cost of the ordinary DDR5 chips it buys. Its annual report states that "There is currently a worldwide shortage of semiconductor, memory and other electronic components driven by the proliferation of AI infrastructure" 2, and its quarterly filing describes "significant constraints in the global supply of semiconductors, particularly memory components, driven in part by demand associated with the buildout of AI infrastructure" 6.

11.Who buys it and why

SimpleStart here

Corsair sells to people building or upgrading their own computers, and to the shops that serve them. Its own segment definitions say what goes with what 2:

SegmentWhat is in it2025 revenue
Gaming Components and SystemsPower supplies, cooling, cases, "dynamic random access memory ('DRAM') modules", prebuilt and custom gaming PCs, laptops and AI workstations 2$980.3m, up 16% 3
Gamer and Creator PeripheralsKeyboards, mice, headsets, controllers, capture cards, Stream Decks, microphones, cameras, sim-racing products and furniture 2$492.1m, up 4% 3

Memory is a third of the whole company — 32.3% of net revenue in 2025 2, which on 2025's $1.47bn works out at about $476m 3. That puts it just behind the entire Gamer and Creator Peripherals segment, at $492.1m 3. By the first half of 2026 the product-category tables had memory ahead: Memory products $272.6m against Gaming Peripherals products $239.2m 6.

DeeperThe detail

The customer list is short, and Corsair discloses how short. "Sales to our ten largest customers accounted for 49.3% and 53.1% of our net revenue in 2025 and 2024, respectively", and Amazon alone accounted for 27.4% of net revenue in 2025 and 30.9% in 2024 2. So slightly over a quarter of Corsair's business goes through a single retailer. The filing adds the exposure that follows: "Our customers, including Amazon, typically do not enter into long-term agreements to purchase our products but instead enter into purchase orders" 2.

The workstation and ECC lines point at a different buyer entirely — professionals running "memory hungry applications such as 3D imaging, video applications, and CAD simulations" on Intel Xeon W or AMD Threadripper PRO 31 — and the segment definition now includes "AI workstations" among the systems Corsair sells 2.

Corsair also tells its buyers, unusually, to check somebody else's list first. Explaining compatibility, it writes that "QVL stands for Qualified Vendor List and it's a vital resource for anyone looking to pair RAM with a motherboard", that "if the memory kit appears on the Memory QVL then it's pretty much guaranteed to work", and it directs readers to the motherboard maker's own support section, describing that route as "by far the most reliable option" 59. Its own tools — a browser-based compatibility checker that takes a system or motherboard model 60 and a downloadable Windows system scanner that "Analyzes current memory & storage usage" and "Recommends compatible upgrade options" 61 — are positioned as the complement, not the authority.

ExpertFor specialists

What memory buyers are actually doing right now is visible in the product-category disclosure, and it is a divergence rather than a trend. In the quarter ended 30 June 2026, Memory products revenue rose to $123.0m from $105.0m a year earlier while Other Component products fell to $75.4m from $112.5m — a 33% decline — and Gaming Peripherals rose to $115.9m from $102.6m 6. Memory gross profit over the same comparison nearly doubled, $28.7m against $16.3m, and across the six months $75.7m against $40.2m 6.

Corsair's own explanation of that split is the most interesting sentence in its recent reporting, because it is a claim about buyer behaviour rather than about Corsair: "Higher memory pricing continued to weigh on the market for DIY PC builds and standalone components, which shrank year-over-year during the quarter", while "Memory revenue grew 17% year-over-year, supported by continued excellence in supply chain management, robust demand and sequential market share gains" 56. Expensive memory, on this account, stops people building whole computers — which hurts the case, cooler and power-supply lines — while the memory itself sells more, at higher prices, to people upgrading what they already own. The company's forward view is that "demand has been deferred, not lost" 56. The same pattern appeared a quarter earlier: memory revenue "grew 6% year over year, supported by prior inventory investments, strong supply chain execution, and sequential market share gains in North America", against "elevated pricing" weighing "on consumer demand" 62.

12.Hidden in plain sight

SimpleStart here

Three things sit in plain sight in Corsair's own documents and are easy to read past.

One. The record gross margin was helped by a tariff refund, not only by the business. Corsair's second-quarter 2026 margin of 33.2% was a company record 56 — and the same quarter's filing reports refunds "totalling $18.1 million including interest earned, of which approximately $15.6 million was recorded as a reduction to cost of goods sold during the second quarter of 2026" 6. Against gross profit of $104.3m for that quarter 27, that is a material one-off.

Two. "Memory products" is a product-category line, not a reportable segment 6 2. The two segments are Gamer and Creator Peripherals and Gaming Components and Systems, and memory is inside the second one along with cases and power supplies.

Three. The same part number can contain different companies' chips, and Corsair says so on its own support page 53.

DeeperThe detail

The tariff story is worth following through, because it runs both ways across two years and explains a lot of margin movement. In the first nine months of 2025 Corsair reported that "the impact of new tariffs on our business was not material to our condensed consolidated financial statements", while warning it expected "the global tariff environment to remain volatile throughout 2025" 5. Its annual report then records that "On February 20, 2026, the U.S. Supreme Court held that the International Emergency Economic Powers Act does not authorize the President of the U.S. to impose tariffs" 2. The refund followed: Corsair "submitted refund claims in April 2026 and ha[s] since received and recognized substantially all of the associated refunds" 6. A company-record margin in the following quarter is therefore partly a legal outcome.

A second thing hiding in plain sight is that Corsair's published decoder is older than its catalogue — three live prefixes are absent from it 52 33 28 38. This is a small thing with a practical edge: a buyer trying to identify a part from the official decoder alone will fail on any CUDIMM, any SHUGO kit and any workstation ECC kit. Laptop modules decode fine.

A third: Corsair describes itself as having "been making performance memory for 30 years" 32, while its own prospectus dates branded gaming memory to 1998 and incorporation to 1994 17. Both statements are Corsair's; they are counting from different events.

ExpertFor specialists

The most consequential quiet disclosure is three words in the supplier sentence: Corsair buys its DRAM chips "largely from third-party distributors" 2. Kingston, the other module brand in this section, qualifies its suppliers directly and names them as suppliers — a purchasing relationship; see that page for the sourcing. Corsair names the same three companies as the producers of the chips it uses, while buying predominantly, though not exclusively, through the distribution channel rather than from them 2. That is a materially different position in a shortage — though read the filing's fallback sentence carefully before overstating it: "Should supply from any of these vendors be limited, there can be no assurance that we would be able to meet our needs by purchasing DRAM ICs produced by other manufacturers or from agents and distributors" 2. That names both routes, so the risk described is an industry-wide shortage rather than a disadvantage of the channel as such. What the channel does change is the lever available: the company that navigated the 2025–26 shortage by "strategically built memory inventory" 56 is describing a buffer it bought early, not an allocation it was granted.

Finally, a governance detail with a real consequence for readers who hold the shares. Because EagleTree controls a majority of the voting power, Corsair relies on Nasdaq's controlled-company exemptions, and states in its own risk factors: "You will not have the same protections afforded to stockholders of companies that are subject to such requirements" 2 10. The company says this about itself; it is not an outside criticism.

13.Weak spots

SimpleStart here

Corsair lists its own weak spots in its annual report, in more direct language than most companies use. The memory-specific ones, in its words 2:

  • Chip prices drive everything. "DRAM integrated circuits ('ICs') account for most of the cost of producing our DRAM modules. The market for these ICs is highly competitive and cyclical."
  • Falling chip prices hurt too. "Declines in the market price of ICs enable our competitors to lower prices and we will likely be forced to lower our product prices in order to compete effectively, which would have an adverse effect on our net revenue."
  • Too much of the business is memory. "A significant portion of our net revenue is generated by sales of DRAM modules and any significant decrease in the average selling prices of our DRAM modules would seriously harm our business."
  • Only three suppliers. "Should supply from any of these vendors be limited, there can be no assurance that we would be able to meet our needs by purchasing DRAM ICs produced by other manufacturers or from agents and distributors."
  • A few customers. Ten customers were 49.3% of 2025 revenue; Amazon alone 27.4%.
  • Concentrated manufacturing. "We rely significantly on manufacturing facilities in Taiwan, China and Vietnam."
DeeperThe detail

The cyclicality risk deserves a second look because it cuts in an unexpected direction. Most readers in 2026 assume high memory prices are Corsair's problem — and they are, for demand: "elevated component costs have made it more difficult for cost-sensitive consumers to upgrade" 6. But the filing is equally worried about the opposite case, stating that "Declines in the prices of these ICs and their related products have tended to have a negative short-term impact on gross margin of our DRAM modules" 2. A module brand holding inventory bought at yesterday's price loses when prices fall and gains when they rise. The record margins of 2026 are the upswing of the same mechanism that will reverse.

The supplier concentration is real but should be stated at the right width. Corsair names three producers — Samsung, Micron and Hynix 2 — which are, between them, most of the world's DRAM output, so this is less a Corsair weakness than the structure of the industry. What is Corsair-specific is the distributor layer — and the filing is careful about it: purchases are made "pursuant to purchase orders and not long-term supply contracts, largely from third-party distributors and, to a lesser extent, directly from those manufacturers" 2. Largely but not only through distributors, and on purchase orders rather than contracts.

On the demand side, the numbers show where the pressure landed. Other Component products — cases, cooling, power supplies — fell 33% year on year in the quarter ended 30 June 2026 6. Whole-system building is what expensive memory suppresses, and that is where Corsair sells the rest of its components.

ExpertFor specialists

Two limits of this page, named so they are not mistaken for findings about the company. First, Item 1A could not be read to its end: every retrieval of the 2025 annual report truncated inside the risk factors, the furthest reaching Item 1C 2. So this section reflects the risk factors that were retrievable, not the complete set, and no claim is made here that Corsair omits any particular risk. Specifically: supplier risk in the retrieved text sits under a supply-chain heading — "Supply chain issues, including scarcity of raw materials or other components to produce our products and limited ability to control or influence our procurement process" — rather than under a heading about reliance on a limited number of suppliers; and no separate Taiwan–China or natural-disaster heading appeared before the fetch truncated, which is a statement about the fetch, not about the filing. Second, the trade-policy risk factor runs to roughly a thousand words and only parts of it were obtained verbatim.

One genuine and specific weakness does belong here, because it is established from pages that were read in full. Corsair's own disclosure that kits sharing a part number may contain different chips 53, combined with the absence of any per-part-number vendor lookup among the pages listed in the sources below, means a buyer who needs a specific die — to match an existing kit, to replace one failed module of four, or because a particular vendor's chips overclock better on their board — cannot reliably order one from Corsair's catalogue. They can only identify what arrived, from the sticker, once it has 53 52. For most buyers this is irrelevant. For the enthusiasts who are Corsair's own stated market — the people buying "Extreme OC PMIC" parts at 9200 MT/s 33 — it is the single most awkward thing about the brand.

15.What’s next

SimpleStart here

Three things are worth watching.

Faster sticks with their own clocks. Corsair's CUDIMM ladder already runs to 9200 MT/s 33, and its own explainer shows operation above 9,000 MT/s on Intel Z890 44. Those speeds are Intel-only in practice, because AMD platforms run a CUDIMM in bypass mode 44.

Memory as a collectible. SHUGO, announced 26 May 2026, is a "limited-edition enthusiast memory series" with patent-pending side-lighting, aimed at builders who treat the machine as "a personal design statement" 21.

Expensive memory, for a while yet. Corsair's own view is that high prices are delaying purchases rather than destroying them: "While elevated memory pricing continues to delay DIY PC builds, we believe that demand has been deferred, not lost, and that Corsair is well positioned to benefit as market conditions normalize" 56.

DeeperThe detail

The company's stated numbers for the year give a sense of how it expects that to play out. Guidance issued in February 2026 was net revenue of $1.33bn to $1.47bn with adjusted EBITDA of $100m to $115m 3; by August it had been raised to $1.40bn to $1.47bn with adjusted EBITDA of $121m to $131m and non-GAAP earnings per share of $0.85 to $0.94 56. So Corsair's own expectation improved across a period in which its component business shrank by a third 6 — memory pricing giving with one hand what it takes with the other.

On supply, the filing describes a deliberate position rather than luck: "The Company's memory supply availability remained adequate as its strategically built memory inventory was substantially right-sized during the first half of 2026" 56. Buying ahead of a shortage is the move available to a brand that purchases through distributors, and it worked here. It also means the inventory cushion has now largely been used.

A form factor to watch that Corsair has explained but not yet shipped: CAMM2 and LPCAMM2 39. Corsair publishes a full explainer and commits only to ensuring readers "have access to the best memory solutions" 39. If laptops and small systems move to compression-attached modules, this is where a Corsair product would appear.

ExpertFor specialists

On records, Corsair's own page claims 12,832 MT/s, achieved by the overclocker "saltycroissant" using a Vengeance DDR5 module that "runs at 7000MT/s stock" — on an ASRock Z890 Taichi OCF with an Intel Core Ultra 7 265K, with 12,250 MT/s cited at ambient temperature against 12,832 under liquid nitrogen 66. That page carries no publication date, which is worth knowing before citing it as current.

Higher figures on Corsair modules have been reported since — 13,530 MT/s on a single 24GB Vengeance DDR5 module in November 2025, on a Gigabyte Z890 Aorus Tachyon ICE with an Intel Core Ultra 9 285K 67. That is press, not a Corsair publication, and is labelled so here: no Corsair page for any figure above 12,832 MT/s was found in this check, which is a statement about what was searched rather than about what exists. The pattern itself is the durable point: extreme memory records now run on module brands' parts and chip makers' dies, and it is the module brand that announces them.

The last thing to watch is structural rather than technical. Corsair's memory business is now the growth engine of a company whose other component lines are shrinking 6, inside a segment where memory sits next to those shrinking lines 2, with a supply position resting predominantly on distributors and on inventory timing rather than on contracted direct allocation 2 56. If memory prices normalise as Corsair expects 56, both halves of that reverse at once: the component lines recover and the memory margins compress. The interesting question for the next few filings is not whether Corsair survives expensive memory — the numbers say it is thriving on it — but what its margins look like when memory gets cheap again, which its own risk factors say is the harder case 2.
