History of Social Media Marketing · 1985–2026

Social media marketing has its roots in the earliest forms of networked community — bulletin board systems in the mid-1980s and the newsgroups of the early internet. The shift from these text-based communities to the visual, algorithm-driven platforms of today represents one of the most significant transformations in marketing history: from one-way broadcast advertising to two-way community participation, from mass audience targeting to interest-graph precision, and from brand-controlled messaging to creator-mediated influence.

Pre-Social: BBS and Usenet

Bulletin Board Systems (BBS) were early dial-up communities where users connected by modem to read and post messages and share files. They were local and text-based, but they established the model of online community that social media later scaled.

Usenet, which dates from around 1980, was a distributed discussion network that pre-dated the World Wide Web, organised into topic “newsgroups”. In April 1994 two immigration lawyers, Canter and Siegel, posted a “Green Card Lottery” advertisement to thousands of newsgroups at once — an early mass commercial posting that set off a lasting argument about advertising in online communities.

Early Social Platforms: 2003–2006

LinkedIn “began in co-founder Reid Hoffman’s living room in 2002 and was officially launched on May 5, 2003” (LinkedIn). Built on real names and professional identities, it was an early professional network, distinct from the pseudonymous communities of earlier internet culture.

MySpace, launched in 2003, became one of the most-visited sites of the mid-2000s. Its customisable profiles and music embedding made it especially important for independent bands, which used it to reach fans without a label.

Facebook started in February 2004 at Harvard and spread to other universities. On 26 September 2006 it opened to “anyone with a valid email address” (Facebook, 26 Sep 2006). That same month it introduced News Feed, which gathered updates from all of a user’s connections into one stream — the feed model that most social platforms now use.

Facebook’s Rise and Business Platform: 2006–2015

Facebook launched Pages for brands on 6 November 2007, with “More than 100,000 new Facebook Pages launched today” (Facebook, 6 Nov 2007). Users who liked a Page received its posts in their News Feed, giving brands an opt-in audience they could reach without paying.

Targeting grew steadily more precise. Facebook says it released Custom Audiences — matching an advertiser’s own customer lists to Facebook accounts — in September 2012 (Facebook, Feb 2013). In October 2015 it replaced its separate conversion-tracking and Custom Audience pixels with a single Facebook pixel (Facebook for Developers, 14 Oct 2015), the basis of website retargeting on the platform.

Facebook’s IPO shares were priced at $38 and were due to begin trading on NASDAQ on 18 May 2012 (Facebook, May 2012), implying a valuation of roughly $104 billion. Pressure to monetise grew after the IPO, and with it the shift from organic to paid reach that shaped Facebook’s relationship with marketers.

Twitter and Real-Time Marketing: 2006–2015

Twitter was released to the public in July 2006 and broke through at the South by Southwest (SXSW) conference in March 2007. Its filing for its 2013 IPO says “Tweets are limited to 140 characters of text” (Twitter S-1, 2013); the limit was raised to 280 characters in 2017. Public-by-default posts made it a public conversation network rather than a private social graph.

The hashtag “came into existence” with a tweet on 23 August 2007 (X blog), proposed by user Chris Messina as a way to group related tweets. It became one of the era’s most useful marketing tools for organising communities and measuring conversation around campaigns. Oreo’s “You can still dunk in the dark” tweet during the 2013 Super Bowl blackout became the standard example of real-time brand marketing.

Instagram and Visual Marketing: 2010–2019

Instagram launched in October 2010 as a mobile-only photo app with filters and grew quickly. Its visual emphasis pushed brand marketing toward curated imagery rather than text updates.

In April 2012 Facebook announced it would buy Instagram for “approximately $1 billion in a combination of cash and shares” (Facebook, Apr 2012). By the time the deal completed in August 2012 its value had fallen with Facebook’s share price: Facebook’s quarterly filing records “a total purchase price of $521 million” (Facebook 10-Q, Q3 2012). Instagram introduced Stories on 2 August 2016 (Instagram, 2 Aug 2016), a format Snapchat had launched in 2013. On its January 2019 earnings call Facebook said “Instagram just passed 500M daily actives on Stories” (Facebook Q4 2018 call).

Influencer marketing on Instagram grew into a large category from around 2015. The legal basis for disclosure was older: in 2009 the FTC’s revised Endorsement Guides said bloggers must disclose “the material connections they share with the seller” (FTC, 5 Oct 2009), and in April 2017 FTC staff “sent out more than 90 letters reminding influencers and marketers” to disclose (FTC, Apr 2017).

Snapchat and the Stories Format: 2011–2020

Snapchat began in 2011 as Picaboo before being renamed (Snap S-1), built around photos and videos that disappeared after viewing. Snap says it “launched Stories in October 2013” (Snap newsroom): a 24-hour sequence of posts visible to followers. Instagram copied the format in August 2016 and Facebook in March 2017 (Facebook, 28 Mar 2017); LinkedIn followed in 2020, as did Twitter with Fleets (X blog, 17 Nov 2020).

Snapchat’s influence on marketing outweighed its audience size. Its S-1 describes “Snap Ads” as “vertical full screen video advertisements” and Lenses as “interactive animations that are overlaid on a person’s face” (Snap S-1) — formats brands used for product try-on and campaign moments.

The Algorithm Feed Era: 2016–2020

The move from chronological to ranked feeds was one of the most consequential changes for marketers. Instagram announced on 15 March 2016 that its feed would be “ordered” by what users were likely to care about, rolling out “in the coming months” (Instagram, 15 Mar 2016). Twitter introduced an option to “Show me the best Tweets first” in February 2016 (X blog, 10 Feb 2016).

Ranked feeds cut brands’ organic reach. In January 2018 Facebook said it would “prioritize posts that spark conversations and meaningful interactions between people” and “prioritize posts from friends and family over public content”, warning that “Pages may see their reach, video watch time and referral traffic decrease” (Facebook, 11 Jan 2018).

TikTok and Short-Form Video: 2017–2023

ByteDance launched TikTok outside China in 2017 and bought Musical.ly in November 2017 for an undisclosed sum. A 2020 US presidential order records that “ByteDance merged its TikTok application with Musical.ly’s social media application” (Federal Register, Aug 2020), which happened in 2018. TikTok’s key idea was the interest graph: showing people content predicted to interest them regardless of whom they followed, so new creators could reach large audiences from zero followers.

In September 2021 TikTok said “More than 1 billion people around the world now come to TikTok every month” (TikTok, 27 Sep 2021). Rivals responded: Instagram introduced Reels in August 2020 (Instagram, 5 Aug 2020), and YouTube began a Shorts beta in India in September 2020 (YouTube, 14 Sep 2020) before rolling it out to more than 100 countries in July 2021 (Google, 12 Jul 2021).

The Creator Economy: 2020–2026

The creator economy — individual creators who build audiences on platforms and earn from brand deals, platform revenue sharing and their own products — became a major category from 2020. Platform programmes such as the YouTube Partner Programme, TikTok’s Creator Fund, Instagram’s Reels bonuses and LinkedIn’s Creator Accelerator turned the relationship into an economic partnership.

The market divides by audience size: macro-influencers (broad reach, high cost), micro-influencers (smaller, niche audiences) and nano-influencers (very small, highly specific communities). Brands increasingly mix tiers rather than relying on a single celebrity endorsement. Published spending totals for the category come from vendors and vary widely, so this guide does not quote one.

Social Media Marketing in 2026

Attention is now spread across TikTok, Instagram, YouTube, LinkedIn, X, Pinterest, Threads, Bluesky and others, organic reach on established platforms is lower than it was, and most brands run multi-platform strategies rather than relying on one network.

A pattern recurs, in this guide’s reading: a platform offers generous organic reach to attract creators, matures its ranking, then reduces organic reach as it builds paid advertising. Facebook and Instagram followed it; marketers who recognise the pattern can invest early in new platforms and plan for the move to paid media.
