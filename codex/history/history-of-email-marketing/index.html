History of Email Marketing · From Spam to Permission 1971–2026

Email is older than the web, Google and social media, and it is still a core marketing channel in 2026. This history traces how it moved from unsolicited mass mailing to permission-based, segmented and automated communication, and how laws such as CAN-SPAM and the EU and UK consent rules shaped it, with primary sources for each step.

The Origin of Email: 1965–1993

Electronic mail began on shared computers: Tom Van Vleck and Noel Morris wrote a MAIL command for MIT’s CTSS time-sharing system “in the summer of 1965” (Van Vleck). Ray Tomlinson of BBN “sent the first network email in 1971” on ARPANET, the forerunner of the internet, and chose the @ sign to separate the user’s name from the host computer — “‘user@host’ became the standard for email addresses” (Raytheon press release, Apr 2012).

For its first two decades email was used mostly by universities, research labs, government and companies. In this guide’s summary, consumer online services such as CompuServe, Prodigy and America Online later brought email to home users.

The first well-known commercial email came early. On 3 May 1978 Gary Thuerk of Digital Equipment Corporation sent a message promoting the DECSYSTEM-20 computer (Computer History Museum); Guinness World Records says “It was sent to 397 email accounts on the ARPAnet”, though the archived replies show the mail program accepted only 320 addresses and the rest spilled into the message body (Guinness World Records). “The reaction to the message was swift and highly negative,” according to the Computer History Museum, and an officer of the Defense Communications Agency, which ran ARPANET, replied that “appropriate action is being taken to preclude its occurrence again” (archived replies, Brad Templeton’s site) — an early sign of the tension between commercial and community use of email.

Spam Goes Mainstream: 1994–1998

The best-known early spam was not an email. On 12 April 1994 two immigration lawyers, Laurence Canter and Martha Siegel, had an advertisement for Green Card lottery services posted to every Usenet newsgroup (Brad Templeton), breaking Usenet’s norm that posts stay on topic. The backlash was fierce. The pair went on to publish How to Make a Fortune on the Information Superhighway (HarperCollins, 1994) (Computer History Museum).

The word “spam” for unwanted repeated messages comes from Monty Python’s spam sketch, in which the word is repeated over and over; it was used in online communities before it was applied to commercial email (Brad Templeton).

The Spam Crisis: 1999–2003

Consumer email grew fast: AOL alone reported “more than 26 million members at December 31, 2000” (AOL Time Warner 10-K). Sending email in bulk costs very little, so spam paid even when almost nobody replied.

By 2003 the US Congress found that unsolicited commercial email was “currently estimated to account for over half of all electronic mail traffic” (15 U.S.C. 7701). Spam filtering and sender reputation systems developed in response, later joined by domain authentication (SPF, DKIM and DMARC), and legitimate senders found that permission and good list hygiene mattered for reaching the inbox.

CAN-SPAM Act: 2003

The Controlling the Assault of Non-Solicited Pornography And Marketing Act (CAN-SPAM), Public Law 108-187 (govinfo), was signed on 16 December 2003 and took effect on 1 January 2004 (Federal Register, 2005). The FTC’s compliance guide sets out its main rules for commercial email (FTC):

  • no false or misleading header information, and no deceptive subject lines;
  • identify the message as an advertisement (the law exempts messages sent with the recipient’s prior affirmative consent from this one rule, 15 U.S.C. 7704(a)(5)(B));
  • include a valid physical postal address;
  • tell recipients how to opt out, and “honor a recipient’s opt-out request within 10 business days.”

CAN-SPAM is an opt-out law: apart from messages sent to mobile-carrier addresses, for which the FCC requires the recipient’s express prior authorization (FCC rule, Federal Register, Mar 2005), it does not require consent before a commercial email is sent — very different from the EU and UK approach. It is not a free pass for cold lists, though: sending a message that already breaks the rules becomes an “aggravated violation” if the sender knew the address was harvested from a website that promised not to share addresses, or generated by a dictionary attack (15 U.S.C. 7704(b)).

The Permission Marketing Era: 1999–2012

Seth Godin’s book Permission Marketing: Turning Strangers into Friends and Friends into Customers (Simon & Schuster, 1999) argued that marketers should earn the right to send messages by offering value, rather than interrupting people who never asked to hear from them.

The idea matched what email practitioners were finding: lists of people who chose to subscribe and expected the messages tend to be more engaged and easier to deliver than bought or scraped lists. This guide does not quote performance figures for that comparison because those in circulation come from vendors rather than independent sources.

ESP Evolution: From Sending Tools to Marketing Platforms

Email service providers (ESPs) began as tools for managing large lists and sending in volume while protecting sender reputation. Constant Contact was incorporated in August 1995 as Roving Software (Constant Contact S-1); Mailchimp was “Founded in Atlanta in 2001” (Mailchimp).

Email then became part of wider marketing automation. Salesforce agreed to buy ExactTarget “in a transaction valued at approximately $2.5 billion” in June 2013 (Salesforce/ExactTarget joint release (SEC)), and Adobe agreed to buy Marketo “for $4.75 billion” in September 2018 (Adobe). Workflows triggered by behaviour — page visits, product views, abandoned baskets, purchases — increasingly replaced one-off scheduled campaigns.

The Mobile Email Shift

Apple introduced the iPhone in January 2007 with “a rich HTML email client” (Apple, Jan 2007), and as smartphones spread, a growing share of email was read on phones. Industry trackers reported mobile becoming the most common place to open email in the early 2010s; their figures vary and come from vendors, so this guide does not give one.

Mobile reading brought new design rules: responsive templates that fit small screens, larger tap targets for buttons, shorter subject lines, and preheader text that acts as a second subject line in the inbox view.

Consent Rules in the EU and UK: 2002–2026

The rule that marketing emails to individuals need consent comes from Article 13 of the ePrivacy Directive of 2002, as amended by Directive 2009/136/EC (EUR-Lex), which each EU country applies through its own law; in the UK it is regulation 22 of the Privacy and Electronic Communications Regulations 2003 (PECR). The GDPR, which has applied since 25 May 2018 (GDPR, EUR-Lex, Article 99), set the standard for what valid consent looks like.

Under PECR, the ICO says you must not send marketing email to individuals unless “they have specifically consented to electronic mail from you”, with exceptions (ICO):

  • Soft opt-in: existing customers who bought, or negotiated to buy, a similar product or service from you, if “you gave them a simple way to opt out both when you first collected their details and in every message you have sent.”
  • Business addresses: “You can email or text any corporate body”, such as a company or government body; sole traders and some partnerships are treated as individuals. Senders must still identify themselves and give a valid address for opting out.
  • Charities: section 114 of the Data (Use and Access) Act 2025 adds a charitable-purpose soft opt-in to PECR (ICO), in force since 5 February 2026 (SI 2026/82).

Where consent is needed, the ICO’s consent guidance says not to use pre-ticked boxes, to make consent specific, and to keep records of who consented, when and how (ICO).

Modern Email Marketing: 2018–2026

Current practice rests on a few principles: build lists from genuine opt-ins, segment by behaviour and preference, automate messages around the customer lifecycle, and send what is relevant to each person.

Apple announced Mail Privacy Protection for iOS 15 in June 2021 (Apple, Jun 2021). When Protect Mail Activity is on, Apple says it “downloads remote content in the background by default” — remote content includes tracking pixels — “regardless of whether you engage with the email” (Apple). That makes opens reported for those users unreliable, so marketers have shifted towards clicks, conversions and revenue as their main measures.

Email Marketing in 2026

Email remains a core channel because it is owned (the list belongs to the business, not to a platform’s algorithm), nearly universal among internet users, and read in the recipient’s own time. Return-on-investment figures are usually self-reported by marketers: for example, the UK Data & Marketing Association’s Marketer Email Tracker 2021 produced in partnership with the email vendor Validity, reported marketers’ estimates of a return of up to “£38.33 for every £1 spent” (DMA). Treat such numbers as survey estimates; results vary widely by sector.

Effective email programmes in 2026 rely on opt-in first-party data, behavioural segmentation, triggered automation, and authentication. Since 1 February 2024 Google has required senders of more than 5,000 messages a day to Gmail accounts to meet additional authentication and unsubscribe rules (Google).
