Affiliate Compliance & FTC Disclosure · The Legal Framework

Affiliate marketing operates within a legal framework that governs disclosure, data privacy, and promotional standards. Violations can result in regulatory action against advertisers, publishers, or both — and the documented enforcement actions from the FTC, ASA, and data protection authorities make clear that these are real risks with real consequences. This guide covers the complete compliance requirements for affiliate marketing in the US and UK, with reference to the European framework.

Why Disclosure is Non-Negotiable

Consumers weigh a recommendation differently when they know the recommender is paid, which is why disclosure rules exist. In the US, the FTC’s Endorsement Guides (16 CFR Part 255) say that where a connection between endorser and seller “might materially affect the weight or credibility of the endorsement, and that connection is not reasonably expected by the audience, such connection must be disclosed clearly and conspicuously” (16 CFR 255.5). In the UK, CAP Code rule 2.1 requires marketing to be “obviously identifiable” (ASA), and in India ASCI and the CCPA set the rules covered below.

An affiliate commission is such a connection. The FTC’s FAQ tells an affiliate site owner: “You should disclose your relationship to the retailer clearly and conspicuously on your site” (FTC FAQ). Responsibility is not only the publisher’s: in the Teami case the FTC alleged that paid influencers’ posts “failed to disclose adequately” the payments, and the settlement required the company to “maintain a system to monitor and review how endorsers are disclosing those connections” (FTC, 6 March 2020).

FTC Endorsement Guides: The US Standard

The FTC’s Guides Concerning the Use of Endorsements and Testimonials in Advertising are codified at 16 CFR Part 255 and were most recently revised in 2023; the current text includes an example of a blogger using affiliate links (16 CFR Part 255). The FTC’s plain-language companions are its FAQ, “What People Are Asking”, and “Disclosures 101 for Social Media Influencers”.

What counts as a connection. The Guides name “monetary payment or the provision of free or discounted products” among the connections that may need disclosing (16 CFR 255.5). A commission on sales is monetary payment.

Where the disclosure goes. “A single disclosure on your home page won’t be sufficient”, and “any required disclosure should be presented without having to click “more.”” (FTC FAQ). “Disclosures are likely to be missed if they appear only on an ABOUT ME or profile page, at the end of posts or videos, or anywhere that requires a person to click MORE” (FTC, Disclosures 101). Put it in the post itself, close to the recommendation.

Which words. “The FTC doesn’t mandate the specific wording of disclosures.” But it warns that “Consumers might not understand that “affiliate link” means that the person placing the link” is paid, says ““Commissionable link” is probably not a clear disclosure”, and that “The use of “#ambassador” or “partner” is ambiguous and confusing” (FTC FAQ). Disclosures 101 calls terms like “advertisement”, “ad” and “sponsored” clear, and says it is “fine (but not necessary)” to add a hashtag such as #ad or #sponsored (FTC, Disclosures 101).

FTC Disclosure Formats and Placement

Formats consistent with the FTC’s guidance (the exact wording is ours unless quoted):

  • At the top of an article, before the first link: the FTC’s own example is “I get commissions for purchases made through links in this post.” (FTC FAQ)
  • At the link itself: ““Paid link” right next to an affiliate link should be an adequate disclosure of the nature of the link” (FTC FAQ). Avoid “(affiliate link)” on its own, for the reason above.
  • On social media: “#ad” or “#sponsored” where it will be seen, not mixed into a block of hashtags or links at the end (FTC, Disclosures 101).
  • In video: say it and show it in the video itself, not only in the description; the Guides say a disclosure presented in both the visual and audible portions is more likely to be clear and conspicuous (16 CFR 255.0).

Not enough on their own: a disclosure on a home page, About page or profile; one placed at the end of a long post or behind “more”; and a generic “partner” or “#ambassador” with no brand name — the FTC allows a branded form such as “AcmePartner” on space-limited platforms (FTC FAQ; FTC, Disclosures 101).

ASA CAP Code: The UK Standard

In the UK, the Advertising Standards Authority (ASA) enforces the CAP Code, written by the Committee of Advertising Practice, and it applies to affiliate marketing. Rule 2.1 requires that marketing communications are “obviously identifiable”, and the ASA states that “Both the business and the affiliate marketer are responsible under the Code” (ASA, Online Affiliate Marketing).

Use “Ad”, not “affiliate”. The ASA’s affiliate guidance says an identifier such as ‘Ad’ is likely to be needed, and records rulings in which “the term “affiliate” itself was not widely understood by consumers” and in which “the label “a d/affiliate” was not sufficient” (ASA). Its Influencers’ guide lists acceptable labels such as “#Ad”, “#Advert” and “#Advertisement”, and places “Affiliate/ Aff/ Af/ Afflink/ Collab” among the labels it advises against, saying “You should avoid labels such as ‘aff’, ‘af’ and ‘afflink’” because they are unlikely to be familiar to the average person (ASA/CMA Influencers’ guide). For a blog or article, the guidance suggests putting the identifier where consumers see it before clicking through, such as the title (ASA).

The Competition and Markets Authority (CMA) also has powers over hidden advertising under consumer law, and the Influencers’ guide sets out what “Both the ASA and the CMA expect” of labels (ASA/CMA Influencers’ guide). India’s ASCI reaches the same practical answer from its own list of permitted labels, below: an “affiliate” label on its own is not enough in either country.

India: ASCI Labels and CCPA Endorsement Rules

India has two layers. The Advertising Standards Council of India (ASCI) is the industry’s self-regulator; its final Guidelines for Influencer Advertising in Digital Media, issued on 27 May 2021, apply to “commercial messages or advertisements published on or after June 14, 2021” (ASCI, 27 May 2021). The statutory layer is the Central Consumer Protection Authority (CCPA) under the Consumer Protection Act, 2019, which notified the Guidelines for Prevention of Misleading Advertisements and Endorsements for Misleading Advertisements, 2022 on 9 June 2022 (PIB, 8 Feb 2023). This CCPA is India’s consumer regulator, not the California privacy law covered further down.

When disclosure is required. ASCI requires a disclosure label whenever there is a “material connection” between advertiser and influencer, defined to include “monetary or other compensation”, free products, discounts and gifts, and its definition of digital media includes “paid-for links” (ASCI). The Department of Consumer Affairs’ “Endorsements Know-hows!” (20 January 2023) says any celebrity, influencer or virtual influencer who can influence their audience’s purchasing decisions “must disclose any material connection with the advertiser”, and that disclosures “must be prominently and clearly displayed” (PIB, 20 Jan 2023). None of ASCI’s documents (the draft, the final guidelines or the FAQ), nor the government releases, uses the word “affiliate”. Our reading: a commission paid by the advertiser is compensation, so an affiliate link is a material connection and needs a label.

Which label. ASCI’s FAQ lists the labels that can be used — “#Advertisement, #Ad, #Sponsored, #Collaboration, #Partnership, #Employee, #Free gift” — and adds “No other labels can be used”, while noting the list will be periodically reviewed (ASCI FAQ). “#Affiliate” is not on the list, so a label that reads only “affiliate” is not one of ASCI’s permitted labels; “#Ad” or “#Sponsored” are. The government release says terms such as “advertisement,” “sponsored,” or “paid promotion” can be used (PIB). The FAQ also says the disclosure “should be in English or translated into the language of the advertisement”.

Where it goes. ASCI’s final guidelines say disclosures are likely to be missed if they appear only on an “About me” or profile page, in bios, at the end of posts or videos, or anywhere that needs a click on “MORE” (ASCI final). The February 2021 draft also said that for a blog the label should go in the title of the post; the final release does not repeat this (ASCI draft, 22 Feb 2021). For video without a caption, the FAQ sets durations: at least three seconds for videos of 15 seconds or less, at least a third of the length for videos under two minutes, and for longer videos the whole of any section where the brand is mentioned. Live streams need the label at the beginning and end. The final guidelines say the same (ASCI FAQ; ASCI final).

Who is responsible, and the penalty. ASCI places responsibility on the advertiser “and also upon the Influencer”, and says the advertiser shall, where needed, ask the influencer to delete or edit a post (ASCI). Under the statutory rules an endorsement “must reflect the genuine, reasonably current opinion” of the endorser, and for a false or misleading advertisement CCPA “may impose fines on manufacturer or endorser” up to Rs 10 lakh, or Rs 50 lakh for repeated violations, under section 21(2) of the Act (PIB). For an Indian affiliate programme, that means putting the ASCI label list into the publisher agreement and checking it, not leaving it to the publisher.

GDPR and Affiliate Marketing Data

The General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) applies to affiliate marketing data processing in two key areas: tracking cookies and conversion data transmission.

Cookie consent: Affiliate tracking cookies that collect user data require valid consent under GDPR — they are not "strictly necessary" cookies and cannot be set without the user's consent in the EU/UK. Advertisers running affiliate programmes must ensure their cookie consent mechanisms cover affiliate tracking cookies, and that affiliate networks implement consent-based cookie setting. Running affiliate tracking on EU users without valid consent is a GDPR violation — the ICO and other data protection authorities have issued documented fines for cookie consent violations.

Conversion data: The data sent from the advertiser to the network when a conversion occurs — including order IDs, order values, and any customer identifiers — is personal data under GDPR. The advertiser is the data controller; the network is a data processor. A Data Processing Agreement (DPA) between the advertiser and network is required under GDPR Article 28.

California CCPA (Privacy) for US Affiliates

The California Consumer Privacy Act (CCPA, amended by CPRA) grants California residents rights over their personal data — including the right to opt out of the "sale" of their personal information. The sharing of user data with affiliate networks for tracking and attribution purposes may constitute a "sale" under CCPA's broad definition, depending on the commercial arrangement.

Practical CCPA compliance for affiliate programmes: provide a "Do Not Sell or Share My Personal Information" option; honour opt-out requests by ceasing to share tracking data with the network for opted-out users; update privacy policy disclosures to accurately describe affiliate tracking data sharing; and verify that affiliate network agreements include CCPA service provider terms.

Advertiser Liability for Affiliate Non-Compliance

Under 16 CFR 255.1(d), advertisers are subject to liability for misleading endorsements and for failing to disclose unexpected material connections, and an advertiser may be liable even when the endorser is not (16 CFR 255.1(d)). The FTC’s FAQ is blunter: “Your company is ultimately responsible for what others do on your behalf.” It adds: “It’s unrealistic to expect you to be aware of every single statement made by a member of your network. But it’s up to you to make a reasonable effort to know what participants in your network are saying.” (FTC FAQ).

What the rule expects of an advertiser: give endorsers guidance on disclosure, monitor whether they comply, and act on non-compliance. These steps are “not a safe harbor”, but should reduce an advertiser’s odds of facing an enforcement action (16 CFR 255.1(d)). Teami had a written social media policy and, in many instances, contracts requiring advance approval of posts, yet the FTC alleged many posts did not follow it; its write-up warns that “A written policy for influencers without effective monitoring and follow-through” falls short (FTC, Teami). The UK position is the same in substance: “Both the business and the affiliate marketer are responsible under the Code” (ASA).

Building a Compliant Programme

A compliance-first affiliate programme incorporates these elements:

  1. Programme terms that clearly specify disclosure requirements. The programme terms should specify the exact disclosure language required (or a minimum standard), where it must appear, and that it must be present in every piece of affiliate content containing affiliate links.
  2. Publisher acceptance of compliance terms. Publishers must actively accept the programme terms as part of joining — including the disclosure requirements. Acceptance creates a documented record that the publisher was informed of compliance obligations.
  3. Pre-launch review of initial publisher content. For new publishers, review their initial content before it goes live to confirm disclosure compliance. This is most practical for high-value publishers; for network-sourced publishers at scale, periodic sampling is necessary.
  4. Ongoing compliance monitoring. Regular audits of publisher content using a combination of manual review and monitoring tools that identify affiliate links on publisher sites without required disclosures.
  5. Documented response to violations. When non-compliance is identified, document the finding, contact the publisher with specific correction requirements, and if corrections are not made, suspend or terminate the publisher. Document all actions taken.

Ongoing Compliance Monitoring

Compliance monitoring at scale is operationally challenging — a large affiliate programme may have hundreds or thousands of active publishers producing content across many platforms. Practical monitoring approaches:

Network monitoring tools. CJ Affiliate, Awin, and Impact all provide publisher content monitoring tools that can identify affiliate links in publisher content and flag missing disclosures. These tools are imperfect but provide systematic coverage at scale.

Manual sampling. Monthly sampling of top publishers' content — checking 5–10 posts from each of the highest-volume publishers — provides a quality check beyond automated monitoring.

Google Alert monitoring. Setting Google Alerts for affiliate link patterns and the programme's branded terms can surface publishers creating content about the programme that may not have been identified through network monitoring.

Consumer complaint monitoring. Complaints about affiliate publishers' practices often surface through social media, review platforms, or directly to the advertiser's customer service. A monitoring process for affiliate-related complaints enables rapid response.

Documented Enforcement Cases

FTC v. Teami (March 2020). The FTC alleged that Instagram posts by paid celebrities and influencers “failed to disclose adequately that the defendants had paid them”, with disclosures hidden below “more”. The proposed settlement requires the defendants to clearly disclose any unexpected material connection and to maintain a monitoring system; “the $15.2 million judgment will be partially suspended upon the payment of $1 million”, and FTC staff sent warning letters to ten of the celebrities and influencers named in the complaint (FTC, 6 March 2020).

UK ASA rulings. The ASA’s affiliate guidance summarises rulings in which an “affiliate” label, or “a d/affiliate”, was not enough to make an ad identifiable (ASA), and it publishes its rulings on its website (ASA rulings).
